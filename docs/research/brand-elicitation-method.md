# How do brand-identity practitioners actually get this material out of a founder — what are the published instruments, in what order, over how many sittings, and what goes wrong?

Research for [#138](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/138), under [Map: to-brand-identity — specify the skill completely, so the build map decides nothing (#136)](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/136). The question is deliberately about *method*, not output: what a practitioner says, in what order, for how long, and what they report going wrong — as distinct from what they do with the material afterwards, which the sibling tickets own. The sourcing bar is the map's: **a structural claim needs two independent named practitioners with published, citable method**, and where a claim clears that bar it is marked; where it does not, the source is named and the claim is left unscored rather than propped up on a secondary write-up. Wheeler, Neumeier, Aaker, Keller and Collins & Porras are the brand/vision-side sources that count here; agency SEO content is cited nowhere. Sinek is admitted only where a second practitioner has independently operationalised him, which happens once (Knapp's brand sprint) and never as a sole source. Because the map flags brand identity as an unusually polluted literature, this pass deliberately went to the adjacent literatures the ticket named — Kelly's repertory grid, Zaltman's ZMET, means-end laddering, and qualitative interviewing method — and **the ticket's suspicion is confirmed: the brand canon publishes structures, and the interviewing craft is somewhere else.** Where a primary text was not retrievable (Neumeier's *Zag*, Ries & Trout's *Positioning*, Kelly's 1955 volumes, Zaltman & Coulter's 1995 *JAR* paper) it is named as unretrieved and not quoted.

## Q1 — Is there an established elicitation method in the brand-identity literature at all?

**Verdict: the brand canon publishes *structures* far more thoroughly than it publishes *procedures*. Two named brand practitioners publish a founder-facing question set in enough detail to be reused — Alina Wheeler and Jake Knapp — and one more, Collins & Porras, publishes a full workshop instrument that the brand texts then borrow. The actual interviewing craft is not in the brand literature; it is in the adjacent construct-elicitation literature, which the brand literature cites and does not reproduce.**

- **Aaker is a structure, not a procedure.** *Building Strong Brands* specifies what a brand identity must contain — "the brand as a product, an organization, a person, and a symbol", a core identity and an extended identity — and specifies a "self-analysis of one's brand and organization" whose "areas of inquiry" are listed as topics ("The current brand image / The brand heritage / The brand's strengths and weaknesses… / The soul of the brand and the organization / Links to other brands"). The nearest thing to a script is a paragraph of example questions per topic, e.g. for brand heritage: "Who were the [early] pioneers of the brand? How did it originate? What was its image when it first started?"; and for soul: "What is the soul of the brand and the organization? What is the brand vision? The dream? … Some very basic introspection can be difficult but illuminating" ([Building Strong Brands](https://archive.org/details/m-david-a.-aaker-building-strong-brands), ch. 3 and ch. 6). Aaker's own summary of the BI model on his firm's site is explicit that it is deliberately unprescriptive: "The BI model does not pre-specify dimensions that all brands in all contexts must contain" ([Six Principles Behind the BI Model](https://prophet.com/2012/02/77-building-brands-six-principles-behind-the-bi-model/)).
- **Keller's published instrument is an audit, not an elicitation.** The Brand Report Card is ten characteristics, each with a verbatim block of diagnostic questions — "Have you attempted to uncover unmet consumer needs and wants? By what methods?", "Have you established necessary and competitive points of parity with competitors? Have you established desirable and deliverable points of difference?" ([The Brand Report Card](https://hbr.org/2000/01/the-brand-report-card), HBR Jan–Feb 2000; text read from the [HBR reprint R00104](https://bbk12e1-cdn.myschoolcdn.com/ftpimages/21/misc/misc_114313.pdf)). It scores an existing brand; it does not get material out of a founder who has none. Its one procedural contribution is naming the two-stage research shape it presumes — a "brand inventory" followed by a "brand exploratory" — with the Disney case as the worked example.
- **The positioning classics contribute almost nothing to elicitation.** Ries & Trout's six questions ("What position do you own? What position do you want to own? Whom must you outgun? Do you have enough money? Can you stick it out? Do you match your position?") are widely reproduced but I could not retrieve *Positioning*'s primary text to verify the wording, and the questions are in any case a strategy checklist rather than an interview protocol. **Named and not scored.** The same applies to Kapferer's identity prism: six facets, no published procedure for filling them.
- **What the brand literature does supply is the failure taxonomy** (Q6) and the *order* of the work (Q3). Both clear the two-source bar. What it does not supply is the sentence the interviewer says.

## Q2 — The question sets and exercises published in enough detail to be reused, reproduced

**Verdict: four instruments clear the reuse bar on the brand/vision side. Wheeler's stakeholder interview is the only published *interview*; Knapp's brand sprint is the only published *founder workshop*; Collins & Porras's vision framework is the only published *admissibility test*; and Neumeier's onliness sentence is the only published *forced-differentiation* form. All four are reproduced here verbatim.**

### Wheeler — the core interview questions, and the rules around them

From [*Designing Brand Identity*, 4th ed.](https://archive.org/details/alina-wheeler-designing-brand-identity-an-essential-guide-for-the-whole-branding) (Wiley), Phase 1, "Interviewing key stakeholders". Wheeler's list, verbatim and in her order:

> What business are you in?  
> How do you market your product and services?  
> What is your mission? What are your three most important goals?  
> What are the trends and changes that affect your industry?  
> Why was this company created?  
> Where will you be in five years? In ten years?  
> Describe your products or services.  
> How do you measure success?  
> Who is your target market?  
> Prioritize your stakeholders in order of importance. How do you want to be perceived by each audience?  
> What values and beliefs unify your employees and drive their performance?  
> What are the potential barriers to the success of your product or service?  
> What is your competitive advantage? Why do your customers choose your product or service? What do you do better than anyone else?  
> Who is your competition? Is there a competitor that you admire most? If so, why?  
> What keeps you up at night?  
> Place yourself in the future. If your company could do anything or be anything, what would it be?  
> If you could communicate a single message about your company, what would it be?

The procedural rules Wheeler attaches to that list matter more than the list, and three of them are directly reusable:

> Interviewing key management is best done face-to-face. Recording the interview facilitates eye contact and a better interview. … Building trust is another agenda. The quality of the questions and the rapport established in the interview set the tone for an important relationship. Encourage individuals to be brief and succinct. **Do not provide questions in advance, if possible, since spontaneous answers may be more insightful.**

> It is absolutely critical for you to read through the baseline information about the company before conducting any interview. It is important to convey that you have already examined the documents provided. The list of who should be interviewed is cocreated with a client. **It is best to keep interviews under forty-five minutes in length.** The following questions should be customized before the interview.

The "baseline information to request" — read *before* any interview, per the rule above — is itemised: "Mission / Vision / Values statement / CEO speeches / Press releases / Value proposition / Organization chart / History / Business plans / Marketing plans / Annual reports / Existing marketing research / Cultural assessments / Employee surveys / Strategic planning documents / News clippings / Domains / Intranet access".

Wheeler publishes a **second**, sharper question set for Phase 2, "Narrowing the focus / Uncover brand essence (or simple truth)", verbatim:

> What does a company do that is best in world? Why do its customers choose it over its competition? What business are they in? **How is it really different than its most successful competitor?** What are three adjectives that summarize how this company wants to be perceived? What are its strengths and weaknesses?

### Knapp — the three-hour Brand Sprint

Jake Knapp's [Three-Hour Brand Sprint](https://library.gv.com/the-three-hour-brand-sprint-3ccabf4b768a) (GV Library) is the only founder-facing brand workshop found with published per-exercise timings. *The GV Library page is served from Medium and returns 403 to automated retrieval; the exercise list, ordering, timings, slider pairs and landscape axes below were read directly from Knapp's own [remote Brand Sprint slide template](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1mCKVYn8YhFAXNmCZ5T7aMnLBVgGeXLnKMalkmGzbqO0/htmlpresent), which is the artifact the article distributes.* Six exercises, in this order, with the template's compressed remote timings:

1. **20-Year Roadmap** — "What will your business do in 5, 10, 15, 20 years?" — 5 min
2. **What does your company do?** — 3 min
3. **How do you do it? What's your secret sauce?** — 3 min
4. **Why does your company exist?** — 3 min
5. **Top 3 Values and Beliefs** — 5 min + 2 min vote
6. **Top 3 Audiences** — 2 min
7. **Personality Sliders** — 5 min — five bipolar scales, verbatim: Elite ↔ Mass Appeal; Serious ↔ Playful; Conventional ↔ Rebel; Friend ↔ Authority; Young & Innovative ↔ Mature & Classic
8. **Competition** — 5 min — then **Competitive Landscape**, 2 min, plotted on two axes: modern ↔ classic, and expressive ↔ reserved

Exercises 2–4 are Knapp's operationalisation of Sinek's golden circle; the sprint runs it **What → How → Why**, i.e. deliberately in the reverse of Sinek's own presentation order, starting from the thing the founder can answer without thinking and ending at the thing they cannot.

### Collins & Porras — the vision framework, and its admissibility test

[*Building Your Company's Vision*](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision) (HBR, Sept–Oct 1996) is the source, and [jimcollins.com's Vision Framework worksheet](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf) is the published, timed, reusable instrument. It is the strongest single artifact found on this ticket, because it is the only one that publishes a **pass/fail test an answer must survive**, rather than a prompt.

The **Core Values Breakout Session** is "Estimated Time: 45 minutes". Each candidate value is tested against seven yes/no questions, verbatim:

> If you were to start a new organization, would you build it around this core value regardless of the industry?  
> Would you want your organization to continue to stand for this core value 100 years into the future, no matter what changes occur in the outside world?  
> **Would you want your organization to hold this core value, even if at some point in time it became a competitive disadvantage — even if in some instances the environment penalized the organization for living this core value?**  
> Do you believe that those who do not share this core value — those who breach it consistently — simply do not belong in your organization?  
> Would you personally continue to hold this core value even if you were not rewarded for holding it?  
> Would you change jobs before giving up this core value?  
> If you awoke tomorrow with more than enough money to retire comfortably for the rest of your life, would you continue to apply this core value to your productive activities?

The worksheet's scoring rule and its stated reason are equally reusable:

> Remember, the only items that truly qualify as core values as those that meet all of the above test questions with a resounding and unqualified "Yes!" **especially the question "would you continue to hold it if it were a strategic disadvantage?"**

> Do a final check on the core values to ensure that none of them fall into the category of "aspiration for the future" rather than authentic core values. Note: people frequently confuse timeless core values — what you truly believe and have always believed at a deep core level — with aspirations of what you'd like to see the organization become in the future. … Do not mix future aspirations into your true and authentic core values, as this will create justifiable cynicism and destroy the power of your core values. **For example, a group that has never held innovation as a core value should not put innovation into its list of core values, even if it sees innovation as a vital strategy for its future.**

The **Core Purpose Breakout Session** is also "Estimated Time: 45 minutes", with its own seven-question test, of which two are load-bearing here:

> Does the purpose help you to decide what activities to **not** pursue, to eliminate from consideration?  
> Is this purpose authentic — something true to what the organization is all about — not merely words on paper that "sound nice"?  
> Would this purpose be greeted with **enthusiasm rather than cynicism** by a broad base of people in the organization?

And both sessions carry the same framing instruction: "**KEEP IN MIND: this is not a wordsmithing exercise.** This is an exercise to capture the authentic core values and purpose of your organization, not to create a 'pretty statement.'"

### Neumeier — the three focus questions and the onliness sentence

Neumeier's own [*The Brand Gap* deck](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/the-brand-gap-2318215/2318215) — the presentation he published himself, and the primary form of the argument — poses three questions as the differentiation test: "**Who are you? What do you do? Why does it matter?**" He restates the same three in his own words in a published interview: "There are three questions you have to answer to be a good brander. Who are we? What do we do? And, why does it matter?" ([Masters of Growth: Understanding Brand — with Marty Neumeier](https://learn.marsdd.com/article/masters-of-growth-understanding-brand-with-marty-neumeier/), MaRS). In the same interview he names the two later instruments in his own voice: the **Brand Commitment Matrix**, "six boxes that you fill in and if you get those right, and they work for you, you can use that as a filter for all your decisions going forward", and "your value proposition, or what I call **'onliness'** of a company".

The onliness sentence itself — "Our brand is the ONLY \_\_\_\_ that \_\_\_\_", category in the first blank, differentiator in the second — is published in *Zag* (New Riders, 2007). **I could not retrieve *Zag*'s primary text**; the formula is therefore recorded here as Neumeier's, corroborated by his own use of the term "onliness" in the MaRS interview and by Wheeler's book independently crediting his "Brand Strategy Cards" as a first-party instrument, but the exact printed sentence is **not verified against the book** and should not be quoted as such.

### The opening triad, independently arrived at three times

The one question set that appears in three independent practitioners' published method, in near-identical words, is the opening triad:

- Neumeier: "**Who are you? What do you do? Why does it matter?**" ([The Brand Gap](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/the-brand-gap-2318215/2318215); restated in his own words in the [MaRS interview](https://learn.marsdd.com/article/masters-of-growth-understanding-brand-with-marty-neumeier/)).
- Michael Johnson (Johnson Banks), describing how *Branding: In Five and a Half Steps* was built: "stripping out all the jargon and just asking simple questions – '**what do you do?**' '**what makes you different?**' and '**why are you here?**'" ([It's Nice That interview](https://www.itsnicethat.com/news/michael-johnson-branding-in-five-and-half-steps-book-260916)).
- Knapp: "What does your company do?" / "How do you do it? What's your secret sauce?" / "Why does your company exist?" ([Brand Sprint template](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1mCKVYn8YhFAXNmCZ5T7aMnLBVgGeXLnKMalkmGzbqO0/htmlpresent)).

**Clears the two-source bar comfortably** — and it is also the least interesting finding on this ticket, because the triad is the part a founder can already answer. Its value is as a warm-up that produces the material the later, harder instruments operate *on*; treated as the interview itself, it is precisely the shape that returns marketing copy.

## Q3 — The sequence, and the stated reason for it

**Verdict: four independent sources give the same shape and, remarkably, the same justification — start where the founder can answer without preparing, and put the comparison against competitors *last*, because a competitor comparison offered early becomes the answer to everything after it. The second convergence is that the interviewer reads the founder's existing material *before* the session and never puts it in front of them during it.**

- **Wheeler puts document review before the interview and the competitor comparison after it.** "Requesting the appropriate information from a client is the first step; it should precede interviewing of any key management or stakeholders." Then: "It is absolutely critical for you to read through the baseline information about the company before conducting any interview." Her Phase 1 ends with an **audit readout** — "a formal presentation made to the key decision makers that synthesizes key learnings from the interviews, research, and audits" — which is a gate, not a summary: the readout "signals the end of the research and analysis phase" and Phase 2's positioning work does not begin until it has landed. Her stated reason for the phase discipline is explicit: "The process is defined by distinct phases with logical beginnings and endpoints, which facilitate decision making at the appropriate intervals. **Eliminating steps or reorganizing the process might present an appealing way to cut costs and time, but doing so can pose substantial risks and impede long-term benefits**" ([Designing Brand Identity](https://archive.org/details/alina-wheeler-designing-brand-identity-an-essential-guide-for-the-whole-branding)).
- **Knapp's brand sprint runs motivation → detail → comparison, with Competitive Landscape as the last of six.** The order is 20-Year Roadmap, What/How/Why, Top 3 Values, Top 3 Audiences, Personality Sliders, Competitive Landscape ([Three-Hour Brand Sprint](https://library.gv.com/the-three-hour-brand-sprint-3ccabf4b768a), read from Knapp's own [template](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1mCKVYn8YhFAXNmCZ5T7aMnLBVgGeXLnKMalkmGzbqO0/htmlpresent)). Within What/How/Why the order is inverted relative to Sinek's own telling — What first, Why last — which is the same principle at question scale: the easy answer buys the founder's way into the hard one.
- **Aaker's strategic brand analysis is internal-first, and he says why.** "In the first phase, the brand is analyzed using existing internal information… those in the organization who have knowledge about the brand and its market should perform a thorough brand strategy analysis with the information at hand. The result should be the identification of identity options". "In the second phase, information can be gathered using a variety of sources and methods including original customer research. **The goal is to fill information gaps**". "In the third phase, the target brand identity, value proposition, brand-customer relationship, and brand position are specified. In this phase, another set of customer studies may be useful for testing and refinement" ([Building Strong Brands](https://archive.org/details/m-david-a.-aaker-building-strong-brands)). The sequencing rationale is a budget argument about research: you do not go and ask anyone anything until you know what you are missing.
- **Collins & Porras sequence from the individual to the organisation, and name that as the mechanism.** "The secret is to **work from the individual to the organization**. People involved in articulating the core values need to answer several questions: What core values do you personally bring to your work? … What would you tell your children are the core values that you hold at work…?" ([Building Your Company's Vision](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision)). The worksheet enforces it procedurally: each participant writes alone, reads out, then the group converges — and in the purpose session, "you cannot vote for or advocate your own; you must advocate a purpose developed by one of the other group members" ([Vision Framework](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf)).
- **ZMET's sequence is explicitly not fixed, which is itself the finding.** The published protocol runs storytelling → missed images → sorting → triadic construct elicitation → most representative image → opposite images → sensory metaphors → mental map → summary image ([Coulter & Zaltman 1994, ACR](https://web.archive.org/web/20231208085910/https://www.acrwebsite.org/volumes/7644/volumes/v21/NA-21)) — but "**every application of the ZMET steps varies since different situations call for different configurations of the sequence of steps**", and "Each step can be introduced in different ways and individual differences among customers may call for somewhat different approaches" (same source, and [Vorell 2003](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=miami1059150389&disposition=inline) reproducing it). The one ordering constant is that storytelling comes first and interpretation comes last: sorting "is used as precursor to Step 4, the further elicitation of constructs using probing interviewing" ([US Patent 5,436,830](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5436830A/en)).
- **The repertory-grid literature adds the ordering rule the brand texts omit: elements before constructs, and the elements determine what you get.** "**The elements used in grid applications (albeit supplied or elicited) determine the type of constructs you will elicit from your interview respondents.** Because elements are so central to grid work, much care must be taken in identifying / eliciting them" ([PCP Encyclopaedia, Element selection](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/element-select.html)). This is the sharpest available warning against a brand interview that opens by naming the competitors: the comparison set *is* the instrument, and choosing it for the founder chooses their answer.

## Q4 — Instruments designed to get past a stated answer

**Verdict: this is where the adjacent literature is decisively stronger than the brand literature, and the finding is structural rather than a list. Every instrument that works does the same thing: it removes the option of answering "what do you think?" and replaces it with either a forced comparison or a forced negation. Nothing in the corpus gets past a stated answer by asking a better direct question.**

### The strongest reusable instrument: triadic elicitation

Kelly's Role Construct Repertory Test asks the respondent to compare three things at a time and name the axis on which two are alike and the third differs. Published forms of the question:

> "the reptest requires the respondent to compare and contrast successive sets of three significant people (e.g., my mother, my father, and myself), and formulate **some important way in which two of the figures are alike, and different from the third**." — Robert A. Neimeyer, [Repertory grid methods](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/repgrid-methods.html), *Internet Encyclopaedia of Personal Construct Psychology*

> "**Think of an important way in which two of those people are alike, and different from the third. Write this characteristic in the column marked 'Emergent'.** … **Then, think of the opposite of that characteristic, and write that in the column marked 'Implicit'. The opposite of the emergent might not be what you would find in a dictionary.** … Whatever you think: That's the point of the exercise." — John F. Kihlstrom, [George Kelly's Role Construct Repertory Test: A Demonstration](https://www.ocf.berkeley.edu/~jfkihlstrom/SocialCognitionWeb/Personality/Kelly_RepTest_Demonstration.pdf), UC Berkeley

> "**Triadic elicitation, or triading, is the most common method to elicit constructs in the RGT.** In this method, the researcher presents three random elements to the participant and asks the participant how two of the elements are similar to each other and different from the third. Once a bipolar distinction between the three elements is made (e.g., fast–slow), that distinction becomes a construct on which all other elements are rated. **All personal constructs require both the left- and right-hand poles.**" — Curtis, Wells, Lowry & Higbee, [An Overview and Tutorial of the Repertory Grid Technique in Information Systems Research](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol23/iss1/3/), *CAIS* 23(3), 2008

Four properties make this the single most transferable instrument found on this ticket, and each is separately documented:

1. **It is bipolar by construction.** A construct is not admissible until both poles are named. That is the mechanism that stops "we value quality": the interviewee must say what the other end of that axis is, and if the other end is "we value shoddiness" the construct collapses on the spot. The literature even names the defect: "**Bent constructs are constructs that do not contrast two opposites and may not be mutually exclusive** … The construct happy-confused, however, could be a mix of two constructs" (CAIS).
2. **Triads beat pairs, and the reason is published.** "Constructs elicited through triads have greater variation, are better able to discriminate among many elements, and generally produce results that are more cognitively complex … **in part because they are elicited through comparison of both similarities and differences. Dyadic elicitation focuses only on differences**" (CAIS, citing Caputi & Reddy 1999).
3. **There is a real methodological fork on how to get the second pole**, and it maps directly onto the map's reversal-test hypothesis. "Using the **difference method**, the participant would then identify how the third element is different from the other two. Using the **opposites method** the participant is prompted to identify the opposite of the label used to describe the first two elements." The literature's preference is for the difference method, and the reason is exactly the one a reversal test has to answer: "**using the difference method, each pole of the bipolar construct is sure to apply to at least one of the elements in the set** … For these reasons, many researchers prefer the difference method to the opposite method" (CAIS). In brand terms: *don't* ask the founder to invent the opposite of their claim; make them find a real competitor the claim does not fit, and read the opposite off that.
4. **It has a stopping rule that is not a clock.** The researcher "repeats this sorting process **until the participant can no longer think of meaningful distinctions or similarities among triads**" (CAIS).

### Laddering — moving between what was said and why it matters

Laddering is the standard companion to triadic elicitation and both ZMET and the repertory-grid tutorials cite the same origin (Hinkle 1965; Bannister & Mair's coinage; Reynolds & Gutman for the marketing form). The community's own reference work is candid that there is no fixed script:

> "**There are no formal instructions for the laddering process** but it basically involves asking **why a person would prefer to be described by one pole of a personal construct rather than the other**. The construct 'ladder' usually ends up with a statement of the values that underlie a person's construing of their personal world." — [Laddering](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/laddering.html), *Internet Encyclopaedia of PCP*

The published example probes, verbatim, are three directions rather than one:

> **Down:** "What characteristics of the program make it more or less friendly?"  
> **Up:** "Why is that distinction important to you?"  
> **Sideways:** "This construct describes the structural layout of the program. What other pertinent attributes can you think of that describe structural layout?"  
> — Curtis et al., [CAIS 23(3)](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol23/iss1/3/)

The stated trigger for laddering is exactly the mush problem: "Laddering can be especially useful in understanding **evaluative but ambiguous constructs such as friendly–less friendly. This construct, while evaluative, gives the researcher little actionable insight**" (CAIS). Note also the vocabulary warning: downward laddering has a different name in the source literature — Landfield called it **pyramiding** — so "laddering" without a direction is ambiguous ([PCP Encyclopaedia](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/laddering.html)).

### Laddering as the brand literature's own missing craft — Reynolds & Gutman

The means-end laddering paper is the one methods text that treats the *conduct* of the interview as the object of study rather than an aside, and three of its instructions transfer to a founder interview unchanged. Reynolds & Gutman, [Laddering Theory, Method, Analysis, and Interpretation](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00218499.1988.12467766), *Journal of Advertising Research* 28(1), 1988 — *the article is paywalled at the publisher; the passages below were read from a [third-party-hosted copy of the article text](https://docsbay.net/doc/482839/laddering-theory-method-analysis-and-interpretation), the same caveat the sibling research docs applied to their hosted book chapters, and the hosted copy is truncated part-way through the interviewing section, so the paper's full inventory of stall-handling probes is **named and not reproduced here***.

- **The atmosphere is engineered before any probing starts.** The interviewer establishes that "**there are no right or wrong answers**", positions themselves as "**a trained facilitator of this discovery process**" rather than an authority, and deliberately takes on a little vulnerability by warning that "**many of the questions may seem somewhat obvious and possibly even stupid**", attributing this to the interviewing guidelines they have to follow. The paper insists "**rapport be established before the actual in-depth probing is initiated as well as maintained during the course of the interview**", with the interviewer neutral in nonverbal cues and withholding approval or disapproval.
- **The core probe is one sentence, asked repeatedly:** some version of "**Why is that important to you?**"
- **The elicitation front-end is, again, Kelly's triads.** Reynolds & Gutman name **triadic sorting (Kelly, 1955)** as one of three general elicitation methods, and their sequence within it is worth copying exactly: the respondent is given three products, names a meaningful distinction, **confirms the bipolarity of that distinction**, states which pole they prefer, and only then is asked why that pole matters. The bipolarity confirmation is a discrete step, not an assumption.

This is a **third** independent literature — after personal construct psychology and ZMET — arriving at triadic comparison as the way into a person's real vocabulary. On the specific question of getting past a stated answer, **that convergence is the strongest finding on this ticket**, and it clears the two-source bar several times over.

### ZMET — the projective apparatus, and what it is actually for

ZMET is the largest published projective instrument in commercial research, it is patented, and its steps are printed in full. Its rationale is stated as a claim about what verbal questioning cannot reach:

> "customers 'say' and 'hear' a great deal more through nonverbal rather than verbal means of communication. However, virtually all market research tools rely on verbal means of communication such as questionnaires, telephone interviews, face-to-face interviews and discussions or focus groups. … **It is these meanings which are often missing from market research.**" — [US Patent 5,436,830, "Metaphor elicitation method"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5436830A/en) (Zaltman & Higie/Coulter, granted 1995)

> "**Nearly all of our cognition is below the level of awareness** … How can we surface it, and relate it to how we think of our jobs, an institution, a cause?" — Zaltman, quoted in [The Mind of the Market](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-mind-of-the-market-extending-the-frontiers-of-marketing-thought), HBS Working Knowledge

> "**One is to double check stated beliefs with actual behavior** … These often reveal that **what consumers actually believe or think, as measured by unconscious physical reactions, contradicts what they say when asked directly**." — Zaltman, [The Subconscious Mind of the Consumer](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/the-subconscious-mind-of-the-consumer-and-how-to-reach-it), HBS Working Knowledge

Four ZMET steps are reusable in a text-only founder interview with no images at all:

- **Missed images** — "the interviewer asked Alice if there were any pertinent images that she had not been able to collect". Generalised: *what did you want to say and couldn't find the words for?* ([Coulter & Zaltman 1994](https://web.archive.org/web/20231208085910/https://www.acrwebsite.org/volumes/7644/volumes/v21/NA-21))
- **Opposite images** — "the customer is asked what pictures would **not** reflect the concept" — a negation step that is a formal part of the protocol, not an improvisation ([patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5436830A/en), Step 7).
- **Sensory metaphor with its negation** — the published probe pair is "'**If University X were a taste, what would it taste like?**'. For further information, the researcher would then ask '**What kind of taste would University X not be?**'" ([Vorell 2003](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=miami1059150389&disposition=inline)).
- **Surprise to the company** — "The customer describes **which of his or her feelings or thoughts on the topic a relevant company is least prepared to hear**" ([patent](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5436830A/en), Step 10). Inverted for a founder, this is the sharpest single question in the corpus: *what is true about you that your own marketing is least prepared to say?*

The structural innovation, and the one worth stealing wholesale, is **who supplies the material**: "Because ZMET has customers collect their own pictures, **the customers (not the researchers) are in control of the stimuli** used in the guided conversation. … **Second, requiring customers to collect the stimuli increases the likelihood of uncovering important, but previously unconsidered customer issues**" ([Coulter & Zaltman 1994](https://web.archive.org/web/20231208085910/https://www.acrwebsite.org/volumes/7644/volumes/v21/NA-21)). ZMET names its own debt explicitly — "**The Kelly Repertory grid and laddering techniques are integral components of the technique**" — so ZMET is not an independent confirmation of triadic elicitation, it is an application of it with participant-supplied elements.

### Forced trade-offs in the brand literature

- **Collins & Porras's core-value test is a battery of counterfactuals**, and it is the closest thing in this corpus to an admissibility bar that a stated answer can fail: hold it "**even if at some point in time it became a competitive disadvantage**"; "**Would you change jobs before giving up this core value?**"; "**If you awoke tomorrow with more than enough money to retire comfortably… would you continue to apply this core value?**" ([Vision Framework](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf)). Their **Random Corporate Serial Killer** game is a full projective scenario, published verbatim: suppose a buyer pays more than a fair price, guarantees everyone's job at the same pay, "**Finally, suppose the buyer plans to kill the company after the purchase — its products or services would be discontinued, its operations would be shut down, its brand names would be shelved forever** … Would you accept the offer? Why or why not? **What would be lost if the company ceased to exist?**" Their stated purpose for it is precisely the pre-decided, fluent respondent: "We've found this exercise to be **very powerful for helping hard-nosed, financially focused executives reflect on their organization's deeper reasons for being**" ([Building Your Company's Vision](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision)).
- **Knapp's Personality Sliders** are forced bipolar positioning — Elite ↔ Mass Appeal, Serious ↔ Playful, Conventional ↔ Rebel, Friend ↔ Authority, Young & Innovative ↔ Mature & Classic — and his **Competitive Landscape** is a forced 2×2 placement against named competitors on modern↔classic and expressive↔reserved ([template](https://docs.google.com/presentation/d/1mCKVYn8YhFAXNmCZ5T7aMnLBVgGeXLnKMalkmGzbqO0/htmlpresent)). Structurally these are Kelly's bipolar constructs with the poles supplied rather than elicited — which the repertory-grid literature warns is the more biasing choice ("Elicitation of elements and/or constructs from individuals introduces the least amount of researcher bias into the grid structure", [CAIS](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol23/iss1/3/)) but which is what makes a three-hour session possible.
- **Card sorts with a "you are not" pile.** The Brand Deck (Scott Thomas / Simple.Honest.Work) sorts trait cards into "**You are, You are not, and Does not apply**", then "focus on the 'You are' pile and **narrow it down to 1 – 6 cards**" ([branding.cards](https://branding.cards/)). This is a product rather than a published method and does **not** clear the two-source bar on its own; it is recorded because it is an independent commercial re-derivation of the same forced-negation shape, and because the third pile — *does not apply* — is a device none of the academic instruments have and which a brand interview needs.
- **Roger Martin's "What would have to be true?"** is the instrument for the founder who arrives with the answer: it does not ask them to give up the answer, it asks them to enumerate the conditions under which the answer would be right, and then to identify "**which of those things that must be true feel least likely to be true**" ([Roger Martin](https://rogerlmartin.substack.com/p/2022-08-22_what-would-have-to-be-true-83dac5bd2189html)).

### Projective technique, named and not scored

Mason Haire, [Projective Techniques in Marketing Research](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/002224295001400501), *Journal of Marketing* 14(5), 1950, is the founding demonstration that a direct question returns the socially acceptable answer: two shopping lists identical but for instant (Nescafé) versus traditional (Maxwell House) coffee, with respondents asked to describe the hypothetical shopper's personality and character rather than to state their own view of instant coffee. **The primary text was not retrieved** (SAGE paywall) and the study is therefore named rather than quoted. It is recorded because it is the cleanest available statement of the underlying principle — *ask about a third party, not about the respondent* — and because the corresponding move for a founder interview ("describe the kind of company that would make this choice") is an obvious and untested transposition rather than something the brand literature has already validated.

### Why this all converges, and what it says about the repo's recurring problem

Every working instrument in this corpus shares one property: **it never asks the subject to report a belief.** Kelly asks for a discrimination between three concrete things. ZMET asks for what does *not* represent the topic. Collins asks whether the value survives a specific, costly counterfactual. Knapp asks for a position on a line whose other end is named. Martin asks for the conditions rather than the conclusion.

That is the same discovery this repo has now made twice from the other direction. [ADR 0009](https://github.com/98kb/skills/blob/main/docs/adr/0009-position-altitude-is-a-trade-off-not-a-product-artifact.md) made a position inadmissible without a dated episode; [ADR 0011](https://github.com/98kb/skills/blob/main/docs/adr/0011-conditional-go-the-stance-record-is-the-delta-between-stated-and-evidenced.md) recorded the founder who states "no co-founder, no investor, no employee" while the episode behind it "reaches **only equity**". Kelly's bipolar-construct requirement and Collins's competitive-disadvantage question are two independent, older discoveries of the same fact: a stated disposition is not evidence, and the only reliable way to convert one into the other is to make the subject choose against something concrete.

## Q5 — One sitting or many, and what determines it

**Verdict: the literature is unanimous that a single sitting is a starting point and not an engagement, but it splits cleanly on *what ends a session*, and the split is the useful finding. Two different stopping rules are in play — a clock, and saturation — and which one a source uses predicts whether it treats the interview as one sitting or many.**

### The published durations

| Source | Unit | Duration | Stopping rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Wheeler](https://archive.org/details/alina-wheeler-designing-brand-identity-an-essential-guide-for-the-whole-branding) | one stakeholder interview | "best to keep interviews **under forty-five minutes**" | clock |
| [Wheeler](https://archive.org/details/alina-wheeler-designing-brand-identity-an-essential-guide-for-the-whole-branding) | engagement | five phases, Phase 1 closing on an **audit readout** | gate |
| [Knapp](https://library.gv.com/the-three-hour-brand-sprint-3ccabf4b768a) | whole brand sprint | **3 hours**, six exercises, per-exercise timeboxes of 2–5 min in the remote template | clock |
| [Collins](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf) | each breakout | "**Estimated Time: 45 minutes**" × three (core values, core purpose, BHAG), then a **Mars Group** second pass | gate |
| [Coulter & Zaltman](https://web.archive.org/web/20231208085910/https://www.acrwebsite.org/volumes/7644/volumes/v21/NA-21) | one ZMET interview | "**approximately two hours**", preceded by **7–10 days** of participant homework | mixed |
| [PCP Encyclopaedia](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/element-select.html) / [CAIS](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol23/iss1/3/) | one repertory grid | "a typical grid with 9 elements can take anywhere between **1-2 hours**, solid!"; "**Typical RGT interviews last one to two hours**" | saturation |
| [Aaker](https://archive.org/details/m-david-a.-aaker-building-strong-brands) | engagement | three phases: internal analysis → gap-filling research → specification and testing | gate |

### The two stopping rules

- **Clock.** Wheeler caps the stakeholder interview at forty-five minutes and Knapp caps the whole sprint at three hours. Both are facilitator-side constraints — Wheeler's is about the interviewee's attention and about running many interviews ("Encourage individuals to be brief and succinct"), Knapp's is about getting a decision-capable group into one room at all.
- **Saturation.** The construct-elicitation literature does not end a session on time; it ends when new material stops arriving. ZMET: "the interviewer continued to randomly select three pictures and question Alice **until no new constructs were elicited**" ([Coulter & Zaltman 1994](https://web.archive.org/web/20231208085910/https://www.acrwebsite.org/volumes/7644/volumes/v21/NA-21)). Repertory grid: the researcher "repeats this sorting process **until the participant can no longer think of meaningful distinctions or similarities among triads**", and the empirical saturation point is published — "the amount of new information … **begins to peak once approximately 15 to 20 constructs and a similar number of elements have been sampled**" ([Neimeyer](https://www.pcp-net.org/encyclopaedia/repgrid-methods.html)); "**most salient constructs can be elicited from just 7 to 10 triads**" ([CAIS](https://aisel.aisnet.org/cais/vol23/iss1/3/)). Even so the practitioners flag the human limit: ZMET interviews "average about two hours … At the same time, **the interviewers must be sensitive to the issue of customer fatigue**" ([Coulter & Zaltman](https://web.archive.org/web/20231208085910/https://www.acrwebsite.org/volumes/7644/volumes/v21/NA-21)).

### What actually forces a second sitting

Three distinct mechanisms appear, and they are not the same thing:

1. **Homework between sittings.** ZMET's defining feature is that the participant spends **seven to ten days** gathering material before the interview, and the stated payoff is not convenience: "requiring customers to collect the stimuli **increases the likelihood of uncovering important, but previously unconsidered customer issues**". The interview is one sitting; the *method* is not, because the week of collection is part of the instrument.
2. **A gate between phases.** Wheeler's audit readout and Collins's Mars Group both interpose a deliberate discontinuity: someone must synthesise, and someone must approve, before the next kind of work starts. Wheeler is explicit that collapsing this is the mistake — "Eliminating steps or reorganizing the process might present an appealing way to cut costs and time, but doing so can pose substantial risks".
3. **Sequencing that depends on the previous session's output.** Aaker's three phases are ordered by information dependency: you cannot know which customer research to commission until the internal analysis has produced "identity options", and you cannot test a specified identity until it exists.

**The three-hour sprint is the honest counter-case and should be read as one.** Knapp does not claim the sprint produces a brand; the article's own subtitle is "**GV's Simple Recipe For Getting Started On Your Brand**". Every source that claims to produce a finished identity is multi-session; the only single-session instrument in the corpus explicitly disclaims that.

### The one source that answers the question directly: Seidman's three-interview series

Irving Seidman's *Interviewing as Qualitative Research: A Guide for Researchers in Education and the Social Sciences* (Teachers College Press) is the only source found that treats "one sitting or many" as a design decision with a stated rationale rather than a scheduling detail. *The book is lending-restricted on [archive.org](https://archive.org/details/interviewingasqu0000seid) and could not be read directly; the passages below are quoted from reproductions of the book's text on the [length](https://phantran.net/length-of-in-depth-interviews/) and [spacing](https://phantran.net/spacing-of-in-depth-interviews/) of in-depth interviews, and are corroborated independently by a peer-reviewed methods article that reproduces the structure with page citations to Seidman.*

**Three interviews, not one, each with a different job:**

1. **Focused life history** — "investigates an individual's life history in relation to the phenomenon of interest"
2. **The details of the experience** — "explores an individual's most recent lived experiences"
3. **Reflection on the meaning** — "highlights an individual's reflection on their lived experiences"

— Julie Linsey et al., [A Methodological Roadmap for Phenomenologically Based Interviewing in Engineering Education](https://seejournal.org/articles/10.21061/see.32), *Studies in Engineering Education*, p. 7, citing Seidman.

**Ninety minutes, and the reason is specific:** "anything shorter than 90 minutes for each interview seems too short" for a participant to reconstruct an experience and reflect on its meaning; an hour is bad because it is "a standard unit of time" that has participants watching the clock, while "two hours seems too long to sit at one time"; and running over is a mistake because "**a situation of diminishing returns sets in. Extending the interview causes an unraveling of the interviewer's purpose and of the participant's confidence that the interviewer will do what he or she promised**" (Seidman).

**Three days to a week between them, and the reason is the point:** the interval "**allows time for the participant to mull over the preceding interview but not enough time to lose the connection between the two**", and spreading the series over two to three weeks "reduces the impact of possibly idiosyncratic interviews" — a participant having a bad day contaminates one third of the record rather than all of it (Seidman). The corroborating article states the same window: "The three interviews should be conducted in relatively close proximity to one another, **over the course of two to three weeks, to provide both the opportunity for a sense of continuity, but also, time for reflection between each interview**" ([Linsey et al.](https://seejournal.org/articles/10.21061/see.32), p. 7).

**And one wording rule that belongs in Q4 as much as here:** "the interview is centered on **how** rather than **why**; a focus on *how* allows for the participant to openly describe their experiences, whereas **a focus on *why* would confine the scope of the interview, suggest a preconceived objective, and prevent recollection and reflection on experiences**" ([Linsey et al.](https://seejournal.org/articles/10.21061/see.32), p. 8). This directly contradicts Reynolds & Gutman's "Why is that important to you?", and the contradiction is real rather than apparent: laddering asks *why* about a construct the respondent has just produced under a forced comparison, whereas Seidman is warning against asking *why* about an experience the respondent has not yet described. **The order matters more than the word: get the concrete account first, ladder on it second.**

### What determines it, stated plainly

Three factors, each supported by at least two sources:

- **How much the interviewee has to go and find.** ZMET's seven-to-ten-day homework and Wheeler's "read the baseline information before conducting any interview" are the same insight from opposite ends: material that has to be assembled cannot be assembled in the room.
- **Whether the next question depends on the last answer.** Aaker's phase 2 exists to "fill information gaps" that phase 1 identified; Seidman's interview 3 asks for reflection on material interview 2 produced. Where the dependency is real, one sitting is not a compression, it is a different and worse instrument.
- **Fatigue and saturation, which are different limits.** The construct literature stops on saturation ("until the participant can no longer think of meaningful distinctions"); Seidman and Zaltman both stop on fatigue at roughly the same place — ninety minutes to two hours. When saturation has not arrived by then, the answer is another sitting, not a longer one.

**Net:** the literature treats the elicitation as **many sittings with material gathered in between**, and the single-sitting instruments in the corpus (Knapp's three-hour sprint) explicitly describe themselves as a start rather than a finish. The determinant is not the ambition of the output — it is whether the founder has to *go and get* anything, and whether any later question depends on an earlier answer. For `to-brand-identity`, both are true by construction, which makes this the strongest evidence yet found for the lifecycle ticket that this skill is not a one-shot session.

## Q6 — The failure modes practitioners actually report

**Verdict: all three failure modes named in the ticket are documented, each by at least two independent named practitioners, and a fourth — aspiration smuggled in as fact — is documented more heavily than any of them. Aaker is the only source that gives the failure modes names.**

### The founder gives marketing copy back

- Aaker names this the **brand position trap**: "The brand position trap occurs when the search for a brand identity becomes a search for a brand position, stimulated by a practical need to provide objectives to those developing the communication programs. **The goal then becomes an advertising tag line rather than a brand identity.** … strategists continuously weed out those aspects that they feel are not worth communicating. … **there is often no room to consider brand personality, organizational associations, or brand symbols because they simply do not make the cut when developing a three-word phrase**" ([Building Strong Brands](https://archive.org/details/m-david-a.-aaker-building-strong-brands)). The mechanism he identifies is precise and transfers directly: the moment the founder believes the output is copy, they start pre-filtering for quotability, and everything unquotable is lost before it is said.
- Collins & Porras attack the same failure with an instruction rather than a diagnosis, twice over: "**KEEP IN MIND: this is not a wordsmithing exercise.** This is an exercise to capture the authentic core values and purpose of your organization, not to create a 'pretty statement'"; and the purpose test question "Is this purpose authentic — something true to what the organization is all about — **not merely words on paper that 'sound nice'**?" ([Vision Framework](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf)). Their "Mount Everest Standard" is the operational form: a good answer is one that survives being said a dozen different ways, so wording cannot be where the value is.
- Neumeier's whole definition is built against it: a brand is "a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or organization… **It's not what you say it is. It's what they say it is**" ([The Brand Gap](https://www.slideshare.net/slideshow/the-brand-gap-2318215/2318215)).

**Clears the two-source bar** — three independent named practitioners, and Aaker supplies the causal mechanism.

### The founder describes the category rather than themselves

- Aaker names this the **product-attribute fixation trap**: "The most common trap of all is the product-attribute fixation trap, in which the strategic and tactical management of the brand is focused solely on product attributes. Based in part on the erroneous assumption that those attributes are the only relevant bases for customer decisions and competitive dynamics, the product-attribute fixation trap usually leads to less than optimal strategies and sometimes to damaging blunders." His summary of the cause: "**The failure to distinguish between a product and a brand creates the product-attribute fixation trap**" ([Building Strong Brands](https://archive.org/details/m-david-a.-aaker-building-strong-brands)). He records the same failure a second time as an observed symptom: "In most organizations, employees have a difficult time answering the question, '**What does your brand stand for?**' '**Achieving a 10 percent increase in sales**' (or profitability) — an all-too-typical response — is hardly inspiring."
- Collins & Porras's five-whys worked example is exactly this failure caught and corrected in the room: a market-research company's executive team "first met for several hours and generated the following statement of purpose for their organization: **To provide the best market-research data available**", which is a description of the category; five whys later it was "To contribute to our customers' success by helping them understand their markets" ([Building Your Company's Vision](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision)). Their asphalt-and-gravel example is the same shape: "We make gravel and asphalt products" → "To make people's lives better by improving the quality of man-made structures."
- Neumeier's onliness sentence is a purpose-built trap for it: forcing the word ONLY into the sentence makes a category description ungrammatical.

**Clears the bar.** But note the **direct disagreement** among the sources, which matters for [#140](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/140): Collins & Porras explicitly deny that this material is supposed to differentiate at all — "**the role of core ideology is to guide and inspire, not to differentiate. Two companies can have the same core values or purpose.** Many companies could have the purpose to make technical contributions, but few live it as passionately as Hewlett-Packard." A reversal or onliness test applied to *values* would fail Collins's own examples; applied to *positioning* it is Neumeier's central claim. The two are not talking about the same field of the artifact, and any admissibility bar has to say which.

### The founder has already decided and wants validation

- Roger Martin's **"What would have to be true?"** is the published instrument aimed squarely at this, and his stated reason is the advocacy dynamic itself: "If you focus on 'what the analysis says,' you will only produce fighting and standoffs", whereas if you "focus first on laying out WWHTBT, it can bring management teams closer together", because "**It gives the two sides the chance of coming to agreement on the logic — i.e., the set of things that would have to hold for this to be a choice**" ([What Would Have to be True?](https://rogerlmartin.substack.com/p/2022-08-22_what-would-have-to-be-true-83dac5bd2189html)). The procedural half is the part that binds a pre-decided founder: "**agree in advance (not after the fact) on what data you want to collect and analyze together**."
- Wheeler's counter-move is at the interview level and is one sentence: "**Do not provide questions in advance, if possible, since spontaneous answers may be more insightful**" ([Designing Brand Identity](https://archive.org/details/alina-wheeler-designing-brand-identity-an-essential-guide-for-the-whole-branding)). A founder who has pre-decided arrives with a prepared answer; the countermeasure is to deny them the chance to prepare one.
- The construct-elicitation literature contributes the interviewer-side half of the same problem — the interviewer who confirms. ZMET protocol: "**It was of utmost importance that the researcher refrained from interpreting any of the pictures in order to not influence or limit the participant's explanations**"; and on the mental-map step, "**Before any suggestions were made however, the interviewer reminded the respondent that she had final say if a construct was important enough to write down.** After each suggestion, though, the interviewer checked to make sure that the construct truly made sense to the participant. If it did not, then it was quickly disregarded" ([Vorell 2003](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file/send?accession=miami1059150389&disposition=inline), reproducing the ZMET protocol).

### The fourth failure mode: aspiration recorded as fact

This one is not in the ticket's list and is better documented than any of the three that are.

- Collins & Porras devote a whole worksheet step to it: "**people frequently confuse timeless core values — what you truly believe and have always believed at a deep core level — with aspirations of what you'd like to see the organization become in the future.** … Do not mix future aspirations into your true and authentic core values, as this will create justifiable cynicism and destroy the power of your core values." The HBR article gives the failure's audible signature: the reaction "**we all know that isn't a core value around here!**" ([Building Your Company's Vision](https://hbr.org/1996/09/building-your-companys-vision); [Vision Framework](https://www.jimcollins.com/tools/vision-framework.pdf)).
- Aaker's **brand image trap** is the mirror-image failure — recording what is currently perceived as if it were identity: "the patience, resources, or expertise to go beyond the brand image is lacking, and the brand image becomes the brand identity". His verdict: "**it lets the customer dictate what you are. In short, it is a customer orientation gone amok** … Creating a brand identity is more than finding out what customers say they want. It must also reflect the soul and vision of the brand, what it hopes to achieve." His fourth trap, the **external perspective trap**, is the failure of never asking the internal question at all ([Building Strong Brands](https://archive.org/details/m-david-a.-aaker-building-strong-brands)).

**Clears the bar**, and it is the pair of failures a `to-brand-identity` artifact is most exposed to, because a founder writing their own brand canon sits exactly between them: too far toward aspiration and it is a wish list; too far toward image and it is a survey of current users.

## Bottom line

**The single strongest recommendation: `to-brand-identity`'s interview should be built almost entirely out of forced comparisons and forced negations, and almost not at all out of questions about what the founder thinks.** Three unrelated literatures — Kelly's personal construct psychology, Zaltman's ZMET, and Reynolds & Gutman's means-end laddering — independently converge on the same instrument, **triadic elicitation**: put three concrete things in front of the subject, ask in what important way two are alike and the third different, confirm that the distinction is genuinely bipolar, and only then ladder on it with "why is that important to you?". For a founder that is trivially adaptable and needs no images: the elements are **named real competitors and the founder's own product**, drawn three at a time. It produces exactly the thing "we value quality" cannot survive — a named opposite pole that a real competitor actually occupies. And the literature has already run the experiment the map is about to run: the **difference method is preferred over the opposites method**, because asking a subject to invent the opposite of their own claim produces "bent constructs" that mix two axes, whereas making them find a competitor the claim does not fit guarantees the other pole is real. **That is a direct, sourced correction to the reversal-test hypothesis in [#140](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/140): don't ask "could a competitor sincerely claim the opposite?" — make the founder name the competitor first and read the opposite off them.**

**On one sitting or many, the literature is unanimous and the reason is mechanical, not aspirational.** Every source that claims to produce a finished identity is multi-session (Wheeler's five gated phases, Aaker's three, Collins's breakouts plus a Mars Group pass, Seidman's three interviews of 90 minutes spaced three days to a week apart, ZMET's seven-to-ten-day homework before a two-hour sitting); the only genuinely single-sitting instrument found, Knapp's three-hour brand sprint, explicitly bills itself as a way to *get started*. What forces the second sitting is not ambition but two structural facts: **material the founder has to go and find cannot be found in the room**, and **any question whose form depends on a previous answer cannot be asked in the same session as that answer**. Both hold for a brand canon by construction. The stopping rule differs by instrument type and both should be adopted: the construct-elicitation portion stops on **saturation** ("until the participant can no longer think of meaningful distinctions"), and the sitting as a whole stops on a **clock** at the ninety-minute-to-two-hour mark that Seidman, Zaltman and Wheeler all independently land near.

**Three further findings the build map should not have to rediscover.**

First, **the brand canon is much thinner on elicitation than its reputation suggests, and the direction of its instruments is wrong for this skill.** Aaker's richest elicitation material — brand personality, the dialogue technique, relationship segmentation — is aimed at *customers*, in samples of fifty to a hundred. ZMET and the repertory grid are likewise respondent-facing research instruments. Only Wheeler, Knapp, Collins & Porras, and Neumeier publish anything aimed at the *principal*, and of those only Wheeler publishes an actual interview. A skill that interviews the founder alone is working in a genuinely thin part of the literature, and should say so rather than imply otherwise.

Second, **the failure modes are documented, named, and have a shared cause.** Aaker's four traps — brand image, brand position, external perspective, product-attribute fixation — cover three of the four modes on their own, and Collins & Porras cover the fourth (aspiration recorded as fact) with the sharpest instruction in the corpus: "a group that has never held innovation as a core value should not put innovation into its list of core values, even if it sees innovation as a vital strategy for its future." The shared cause Aaker identifies is worth writing into the skill directly: **the moment the founder believes the output is copy, they begin pre-filtering for quotability, and everything unquotable is lost before it is said.** Collins's countermeasure is the one to copy — say at the top, and mean it, that this is not a wordsmithing exercise.

Third, and most consequential for [#140](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/140): **the sources disagree about whether this material is supposed to differentiate at all, and the disagreement is not a muddle — it is a field boundary.** Neumeier's onliness sentence exists to force differentiation. Collins & Porras flatly deny that differentiation is the job: "the role of core ideology is to guide and inspire, not to differentiate. Two companies can have the same core values or purpose." Both are right about their own object. A reversal or onliness test is the correct admissibility bar for a **positioning** claim and the wrong one for a **value**, where the correct bar is Collins's costliness test — would you hold this even if it became a competitive disadvantage; would you change jobs before giving it up. **The artifact needs two admissibility bars, not one**, and which fields get which is the decision #140 actually has to make.
