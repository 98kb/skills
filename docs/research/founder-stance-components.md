# What are the real components of a documented stance, and which of them inform a decision rather than support an impersonation?

Research for [#82](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/82), under [Map: to-persona Phase 1 (#81)](https://github.com/98kb/skills/issues/81). The artifact under design is a durable record of a founder's stance loaded as *context enrichment* for a human or agent working downstream — not roleplay, not simulation. The three sections below answer #80's Q1 (components, and the decision/impersonation split), Q2 (single-sitting elicitable vs. requiring longitudinal observation), and Q6 (known failure modes). Impersonation prior art — character bibles, acting craft, voice mechanics — was excluded by construction; design personas were *included* precisely because they mix both kinds and read as authoritative, and sorting them is the point. The guessed vocabulary from #80 ("opinions, biases, motivations") was deliberately not used as a search frame; what follows is what practice actually names. Sources are the practitioner's or researcher's own text; secondary write-ups were used only to locate primary URLs and are never cited as evidence.

## Sources consulted

- Alan Cooper, Robert Reimann & Dave Cronin, [*About Face 3*, Ch. 5 "Modeling Users: Personas and Goals"](https://katepedavoli.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/06/w5-cooper-modelling.pdf) — Cooper invented the persona method; this is the method's own text (third-party-hosted copy of the book chapter, same caveat the prior docs applied to the Maurya mirror).
- Nielsen Norman Group, [Personas vs. Archetypes](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/personas-archetypes/) and [Why Personas Fail](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-personas-fail/) — NN/g are the practitioners, not aggregators; the first article is a direct first-party ruling on which parts of a persona carry information.
- Indi Young, [Describing Personas](https://medium.com/@indiyoung/describing-personas-af992e3fc527) — first-party practitioner writing from the originator of "thinking styles"; the only source found that names a decision-instruction component directly.
- Christopher Chapman & Russell Milham, [The Personas' New Clothes](https://cnchapman.files.wordpress.com/2007/03/chapman-milham-personas-hfes2006-0139-0330.pdf) (HFES 2006, author-hosted copy) — the peer-reviewed falsifiability attack on personas.
- Jonathan Baron & Mark Spranca, [Protected Values](https://www.sas.upenn.edu/~baron/papers/pv97.pdf) (*OBHDP* 70(1), 1997, author-hosted) — the originating paper for the negative-space component, with its full elicitation instrument printed in the appendix.
- Philip Tetlock, [Thinking the unthinkable: sacred values and taboo cognitions](https://gwern.net/doc/philosophy/ethics/2003-tetlock.pdf) (*Trends in Cognitive Sciences* 7(7), 2003) — the parallel research programme on values people refuse to trade, by its author.
- Samuel Bond, Kurt Carlson & Ralph Keeney, [Generating Objectives: Can Decision Makers Articulate What They Want?](https://www.scheller.gatech.edu/directory/research/marketing/bond/pdf/bond_carlson_keeney-man-sci-2008.pdf) (*Management Science* 54(1), 2008, author-hosted) — Keeney is value-focused thinking's author; this paper measures how much of a person's own value set survives a single elicitation sitting.
- Richard Nisbett & Timothy Wilson, [Telling More Than We Can Know](https://web.math.princeton.edu/~sswang/literature_general_unsorted/Nisbett-Wilson-PsychologicalReview1977.pdf) (*Psychological Review* 84, 1977) — the primary evidence on whether people can report their own decision reasoning at all.
- David McClelland, [Identifying Competencies with Behavioral-Event Interviews](https://skillup.coreconsulting.it/home/pdfs/McClelland,%20D.%20C.%20(1998).%20Identifying%20competencies%20with%20behavioral-event%20interviews..pdf) (*Psychological Science* 9(5), 1998) — the method text for the one interview shape that the competency literature trusts; descends from Flanagan's critical-incident technique.
- Action Design, [Double-Loop Learning](https://actiondesign.com/resources/readings/double-loop-learning) and [Applying Argyris](https://actiondesign.com/resources/readings/applying-argyris) — the firm of Robert Putnam, co-author with Argyris and Schön of *Action Science*; first-party statement of espoused theory vs. theory-in-use and the method for getting at it.
- Oral History Association, [Core Principles](https://oralhistory.org/oha-core-principles/) and [Best Practices](https://oralhistory.org/best-practices/) — the discipline's own standards for recording one named person's account as a durable primary source.
- Shane Parrish, [How a Decision Journal Changed the Way I Make Decisions](https://fs.blog/decision-journal/) — first-party practitioner writing on recording decision reasoning at decision time.

## Q1 — What are the components, and which inform a decision vs. support an impersonation?

**Verdict: the split is real, and two sources draw it themselves in almost the terms #80 uses — but the decision-informing set is small, and it is assembled from four separate disciplines, none of which has a component called "a stance". The negative-space lead is the strongest single finding: it is a named, instrumented component with a fifty-year literature, called *protected values* or *sacred values*.**

- **Cooper draws the line himself, and puts almost all fictional material on the far side of it.** "A persona without goals can still serve as a useful communication tool, but it remains useless as a design tool" — goals are "the drivers behind those behaviors", and "tasks are only a means to an end; goals are the end themselves" (*About Face 3*, Ch. 5). On the other side: names, cars, family members "are employed sparingly as narrative tools to help better communicate the real data and are not ends in themselves", and "supporting fictional detail plays only the most minor part in persona creation and is used just enough to make the persona come to life in the minds of the designers". His warning is blunt: "Too much fictional, idiosyncratic biography… is a distraction and makes your personas less credible. Remember that you are creating a design tool, not a character sketch for a novel. Only concrete data can support the design and business decisions your team will ultimately make." That last sentence is the split, stated by the method's inventor.
- **NN/g confirm the fictional layer carries no information, by showing it can be deleted losslessly.** With personas "we invent a (plausible) name, bio, photo, and other personal characteristics", whereas archetypes "omit those details and refer to the user type merely by an abstract label" — and yet "personas and archetypes are functionally the same. They represent the same data and insights about our users' behaviors, attitudes, goals, and pain points" (Personas vs. Archetypes). The invented layer's only stated job is empathy and memory: "they put a name, a human face, and *believable* motivations to an otherwise abstract bundle of characteristics." For an artifact loaded as context rather than rallied around by a team, that job does not exist.
- **Indi Young names the decision-informing component more precisely than anyone else found.** She keeps "the reasoning, reactions, and guiding principles" and "the deeper, underlying reasoning behind the preferences and statements-of-fact", and rejects demographics because they "can cause assumptions, shortcuts in thinking, and subconscious stereotypes" (Describing Personas). Her *guiding principles* — described in her own framing as instructions people use for making decisions — is the closest thing in the corpus to the component #80 is reaching for, and it is defined by its use in a decision rather than by its contribution to a portrait.
- **The negative-space component exists, is named, and has an instrument.** Baron & Spranca: "Protected values are those that resist trade-offs with other values… We define a value as protected for a subject when the subject says that the value should not be traded off, i.e., it is absolute" (Protected Values). They give it five further properties — *quantity insensitivity*, *agent relativity*, *moral obligation*, *anger at the thought of making trade-offs*, and *denial of the need for trade-offs through wishful thinking* — each with its own questionnaire item. Tetlock's parallel construct: sacred values are "those values that a moral community treats as possessing transcendental significance that precludes comparisons, trade-offs, or indeed any mingling with secular values", and trading them is a *taboo trade-off* (Thinking the unthinkable). Both are unambiguously decision-informing: they predict which proposals the person will refuse outright rather than price. Neither was built for founders and both are pitched at moral values, so the transfer to product positions is by analogy, not by precedent — but the vocabulary and the instrument are real, which is more than the ticket expected.
- **Objectives, in Keeney's sense, are the fourth decision-informing component** — what the person is trying to achieve, separated into fundamental and means objectives — and Bond, Carlson & Keeney treat them as the basis of a decision rather than a description of a person: "objectives have long been considered a basis for sound decision making" (Generating Objectives).
- **Components that do not clearly land on either side.** Cooper's *life goals* are decision-adjacent at best by his own account: "Life goals rarely figure directly into the design of specific elements of an interface", and he budgets "zero or one life goal… for most personas". *Experience goals* — how someone wants to feel — he treats as near-universal defaults ("don't feel stupid", "don't waste time") that "can be taken as implicit for almost any persona". Young's *reactions* (emotions at a point in time) are load-bearing in her method but their route into a decision is indirect. These three are honestly ambiguous and should not be counted on either side without a further pass.
- **Unambiguously impersonation-supporting, by the sources' own accounts:** name, photograph, biography, demographics, family, possessions, and any voice or manner material. No source in scope claims a decision use for any of it; Cooper and NN/g both explicitly assign it to memorability and team communication.

## Q2 — Which components are elicitable in a single sitting, and which need longitudinal observation?

**Verdict: three of the four decision-informing components are single-sitting elicitable, but only under specific interview shapes — and the one thing every source agrees you cannot get by asking is the person's actual operating rule, as opposed to their account of it. That is the component to name as out of reach rather than quietly drop.**

- **Protected values: yes, one sitting.** Baron & Spranca's Experiment 1 was a single questionnaire: 72 subjects, 12 questions about each of 14 actions, paid $6/hour. The items are directly reusable in shape — "This should be prohibited no matter how great the benefits from allowing it", "It is equally wrong to allow some of this to happen and to allow twice as much to happen. The amount doesn't matter", "It is impossible for me to think about how much benefit we should demand in order to allow this to happen" (Protected Values, appendix). An interview-shaped skill can ask these.
- **Objectives: one sitting, but only with a prompt list.** Bond, Carlson & Keeney's finding is the sharpest constraint in this doc: "participants consistently omitted nearly half of the objectives that they later identified as personally relevant. More surprisingly, omitted objectives were perceived to be almost as important as those generated by participants on their own." Their fix is procedural and cheap — after free generation, show a master list and have the subject check what is relevant, splitting the result into *self-generated* and *recognized* objectives (Generating Objectives). Unaided elicitation in one sitting yields roughly half a stance.
- **Reasoning attached to specific past decisions: one sitting, but only in behavioural-event shape.** McClelland's BEI has subjects "describe, in their own words, what they said, thought, felt, and did in six episodes—three positive and three negative" (Identifying Competencies with Behavioral-Event Interviews). The unit is a named past episode, not a general self-description — which is what makes it survivable given Q3's evidence. Putnam's left-hand-column case is the same move in a different tradition: "you take a piece of paper, draw a line down the middle and on the right you write down what was actually said, as near as you can remember, and in the left-hand column you write any unspoken thoughts or feelings you had" (Applying Argyris).
- **Out of reach for an interview #1 — goals as Cooper defines them.** "You can't ask a person what his goals are directly: Either he won't be able to articulate them, or he won't be accurate or even perfectly honest. People simply aren't well prepared to answer such questions accurately." His method requires reconstructing them "from observed behaviors, answers to other questions, non-verbal cues, and clues from the environment such as book titles on shelves" (*About Face 3*, Ch. 5). A single self-report sitting cannot produce a Cooper goal.
- **Out of reach for an interview #2 — theory-in-use.** Action Design's own definition: theory-in-use is "the theory of action that can be inferred from behavior" (Double-Loop Learning) — inferred, not reported. The espoused/in-use gap is by construction invisible to the person espousing. The left-hand-column case above is the nearest single-sitting proxy, and it works only because it asks for a reconstructed verbatim exchange rather than an opinion.
- **Out of reach for an interview #3 — anything requiring a track record.** Parrish's decision journal is explicit that the record must precede the outcome: "the key is looking in this mirror before you act, not after", because "it's hard to remember what we knew at the time and what we were thinking" (fs.blog). Calibration, consistency across decisions, and stance drift over time are all in this category. They are not elicitable at any interview length; they require the artifact to have been kept, not written.
- **Trivially elicitable but not worth eliciting:** every impersonation component in Q1. Demographics and biography cost nothing to collect, which is exactly why they crowd into personas.

## Q3 — Known failure modes: stereotyping, self-report bias, and describing the person one wishes one were

**Verdict: all three are documented, and the third is the best-attested of the three. One classic persona failure mode does *not* transfer (representativeness stereotyping, because the subject is one named real person) and is replaced by a worse one (over-extension of a real record to questions it never addressed). The non-falsifiability critique transfers intact and lands on the impersonation layer specifically.**

- **Stereotyping.** Cooper: "Stereotypes represent designer or researcher biases and assumptions, rather than factual data. Personas developed drawing on inadequate research… run the risk of degrading to stereotypical caricatures." He identifies the specific failure shape as the demographic sketch — "a name (and possibly a picture) attached to brief, usually demographic data, along with a short, fictional paragraph describing the kind of car this person drives… This kind of user profile is likely to be a user stereotype and is not useful as a design tool" (*About Face 3*, Ch. 5). Young's version is the same finding from the other end: demographics "can cause assumptions, shortcuts in thinking, and subconscious stereotypes". NN/g name the representativeness variant: "we are quite literally saying that *these are our users* – anyone who isn't represented by those faces is therefore being implicitly excluded" (Personas vs. Archetypes). Oral history's standard is the terse form of all of it: "avoiding stereotypes, misrepresentations, and manipulations of the narrator's words" (OHA Best Practices). **Note the asymmetry for this artifact:** with one named real subject, NN/g's exclusion risk mostly evaporates, but Cooper's caricature risk does not — the pressure to make a founder's positions vivid is exactly the pressure that turns them into a caricature of themselves.
- **Self-report bias.** Nisbett & Wilson's conclusion is the hard floor under any interview-shaped skill: when people report on their own cognitive processes "they do not do so on the basis of any true introspection", their answers instead being "based on a priori, implicit causal theories" about what plausibly caused what. Cooper's practitioner-level restatement — "he won't be accurate or even perfectly honest" — was arrived at independently. Bond, Carlson & Keeney add the quantitative version: half the relevant objectives simply do not get said. And Baron & Spranca anticipate the specific self-report failure that attaches to declared non-negotiables, which they call **posturing**: "when people say that their values are absolute, they may sometimes be simply taking a strong negotiating stance, making 'nonnegotiable demands'." They built a control for it — a private condition where responses could not be linked to issues, versus a public one where they could — and two questionnaire items that ask about it directly ("In public discussions of this issue, it is most effective to exaggerate the strength of our opposition to this"). Their result is reassuring but not exonerating: protected values were essentially uncorrelated with the posturing measure (r = .10), "even though items that evoke protected values over all subjects also evoke posturing."
- **Describing the person one wishes one were.** This is the failure mode with the most independent corroboration, and it is structural rather than a matter of honesty. Argyris and Schön's distinction is the general form: espoused theory is what one says one does; theory-in-use is "the theory of action that can be inferred from behavior" (Action Design), and the gap is invisible from the inside. Tetlock names it for exactly the negative-space component: "the policy a community proclaims towards a sacred value represents an expressed, not a revealed, preference. Our actual choices may belie our high-sounding proclamations that we have assigned infinite weight to the sacred value." Worse for our purposes, his reality-constraint hypotheses predict *how* the proclamation fails in practice: people "will welcome rhetorical redefinitions of situations that transform taboo trade-offs into more acceptable routine trade-offs… or tragic trade-offs" once the cost of upholding the value becomes prohibitive. A recorded non-negotiable therefore predicts the founder's outrage at a proposal framed as a taboo trade-off, and predicts very little about the founder's actual choice once someone reframes it. Parrish supplies the memory mechanism: "hindsight makes things far more explainable and changes our story." Oral history supplies the interview-level mechanism: memory "captures recollections about the past filtered through the lens of a changing personal and social context", and "narrators shape the interview based on what they deem to be relevant, meaningful, or appropriate to share" (OHA Core Principles).
- **Non-falsifiability, which lands squarely on the impersonation layer.** Chapman & Milham: "Because personas are admittedly fictional, it would seem that there is no way to falsify them; no data can disprove a fictional construction. Therefore, they are outside the scientific method and cannot be verified." Their representativeness objection ("How many users does a given persona describe?") does not transfer to a single named founder — but the falsifiability objection transfers *and sharpens*, because it identifies the fictional layer as the specific thing that makes the artifact unfalsifiable. This is direct evidence that the impersonation components are not merely inert: they are what would make a stance artifact immune to correction. ## Bottom line

There is no discipline that documents "a founder's stance". The decision-informing component set has to be assembled from four of them, and it comes out **thin — four components, one of which cannot be obtained by asking.** That thinness is the finding, and #81 should weigh it as such rather than pad it.

Candidate components, each with a decision/impersonation verdict and an elicitability verdict:

1. **Protected values / trade-off resistance** — what this person refuses to price at all, plus the five attached properties (quantity insensitivity, agent relativity, moral obligation, anger at trade-offs, denial of the need for them). *Named by* Baron & Spranca and Tetlock. **Decision-informing**, and the strongest of the set: it is the direct answer to #80's negative-space lead, and practice *does* have both a name and an instrument for "positions this person would never take". **Elicitable in one sitting** — a printed questionnaire exists and its items are reusable in interview form — but this is simultaneously the component most exposed to posturing and to the expressed-versus-revealed gap.
2. **Objectives** — what the person is trying to achieve, fundamental separated from means. *Named by* Keeney; Bond, Carlson & Keeney. **Decision-informing.** **Elicitable in one sitting, but only with a prompt list**: free generation alone recovers about half. Ship the master-list step or ship half a stance.
3. **Guiding principles / reasoning** — the instructions the person applies when deciding, and the reasoning under each stated position. *Named by* Indi Young. **Decision-informing.** **Elicitable in one sitting** via listening-session shape. Weakest evidential backing of the four — one practitioner source, no instrument.
4. **Decision episodes** — specific past decisions with what was said, thought, felt and done. *Named by* Flanagan → McClelland (BEI) and Putnam (left-hand column). **Decision-informing**, and also the carrier for everything above: the only self-report form the competency literature trusts. **Elicitable in one sitting** — six episodes, three positive and three negative, is a proven shape.
5. **Goals in Cooper's sense.** **Decision-informing, but not elicitable**: "You can't ask a person what his goals are directly." Requires inference from observed behaviour. Name it as out of scope rather than dropping it.
6. **Theory-in-use** — the rule that actually governed the decisions. *Named by* Argyris & Schön / Action Design. **Decision-informing, and the most valuable of all if you could get it — but not elicitable**: inferred from behaviour, invisible to the subject. The left-hand-column case is the only partial workaround.
7. **Calibration, consistency, drift.** *Named by* Parrish (decision journal) and Tetlock. **Decision-informing but not elicitable at any interview length**: requires records kept at decision time, which is structurally unavailable to a skill that writes the record once.
8. **Life goals, experience goals, emotional reactions.** *Named by* Cooper and Young. **Genuinely unclear which side they fall on** — Cooper says life goals "rarely figure directly" in design decisions and budgets zero-to-one of them. Elicitable, but do not include them without first deciding what they are for.
9. **Name, photo, biography, demographics, possessions, voice and manner.** *Named by* Cooper and NN/g. **Impersonation-supporting**: NN/g demonstrate the layer is losslessly deletable, Cooper calls it "a distraction". Trivially elicitable — which is exactly why it colonises personas. Exclude.

Two things the sources do **not** give, and manufacturing them would be dishonest. First, nobody has validated any of this on a founder recording their own stance for downstream reuse: protected values come from moral-psychology surveys, objectives from decision analysis, BEIs from hiring, personas from designing for strangers. Every transfer here is by analogy. Second, and more sharply: the single most decision-useful component found — the negative-space one — is also the one the evidence most directly undermines, because Tetlock shows a declared absolute is an *expressed* preference that bends under rhetorical reframing, and Baron & Spranca show respondents will admit to inflating exactly this kind of declaration. If Phase 1 keeps only one component, it should keep that one; but it should also record it as *what this founder said they would never trade*, never as *what this founder will never trade*, and that distinction is a design requirement, not a caveat.
