# What do primary sources say about the ritual, decision options, and "what's next" mechanics at a gate/review — for grounding `to-milestone`'s design

Research for `to-milestone`, the fourth skill in the `to-vision → to-pitch → to-roadmap → to-milestone` pipeline. `to-roadmap` already states each Move's **Evidence Threshold** (a signal + a bar, e.g. "10 paying customers from cohort X, ≥10") at authoring/resequencing time — see `CONTEXT.md`'s "Move" and "Evidence Threshold" entries and [ADR 0006](../adr/0006-to-roadmap-artifact-schema-strategic-frame-replaces-wedge.md). `to-milestone`'s job, not yet designed, is to periodically check accumulated evidence against that pre-committed threshold and decide whether to advance to the next Move, persevere on the current one, or trigger a resequencing/pivot conversation (handled separately by `to-roadmap`'s re-entry mode, per the "Re-entry session" term). This doc investigates how four different primary-source traditions structure that same shape of decision — a periodic review that checks real evidence against a pre-committed bar and then acts on the result — so a later design session has primary-source grounding rather than folk description. All sources below are first-party (the practitioner's own book/companion site/blog/official guide) per the same sourcing bar as the prior research docs in this directory; secondary summaries were fetched only to locate primary URLs, never cited as evidence, except where noted as an explicit caveat.

## Shape Up (Basecamp / Ryan Singer) — the betting table and cool-down cycle

**(a) What ritual/artifact records the gate decision.** There is no written verdict artifact — the gate is a live meeting, not a document. Every six-week cycle ends in a two-week, unscheduled **cool-down**: "If we were to run six-week cycles back to back, there wouldn't be any time to breathe and think about what's next" (The Betting Table). During cool-down the **betting table** reconvenes — a small, time-boxed meeting ("C-level time is only available in small slices, so there's an atmosphere of 'waste no time' and the call rarely goes longer than an hour or two," The Betting Table) — to decide what happens next. Nothing downstream of that meeting is described as a formal write-up; the record is the bet placed for the next cycle, not a retrospective document about the last one.

**(b) What the decision options are.** Shape Up's gate is binary by construction, enforced by the **circuit breaker**: "Teams have to ship the work within the amount of time that we bet. If they don't finish, by default the project doesn't get an extension" (The Betting Table). An extension is possible only under two conjunctive conditions, both narrowly scoped: "The outstanding tasks must be true must-haves that withstood every attempt to scope hammer them" and "the outstanding work must be all downhill. No unsolved problems; no open questions" (Decide When to Stop) — i.e., "we're basically done but need a few more days," not "we need another six weeks to find out." There is no formal "persevere" option in the Lean Startup sense — a bet either ships (done, compared "down to baseline — the current reality for customers," not up to an ideal; Decide When to Stop) or it doesn't, and if it doesn't, "it means we did something wrong in the shaping," which "pushes us to reframe the problem" (The Betting Table) rather than re-fund the same bet.

**(c) How "what's next" gets selected.** Selection is explicitly not driven by a standing backlog: "Bets, Not Backlogs" is the chapter title, and the betting table looks only at "pitches from the last six weeks — or any pitches that somebody purposefully revived and lobbied for again" (Place Your Bets, per `roadmap-strategy-bridge-mechanics.md`'s Q3 citation of the same chapter). What gets bet on next is explicitly conditioned by what just happened, but only as one input among several: "The kind of project we want to do next can depend on which projects we've done recently" (Place Your Bets) — Singer names concrete rotation logic (alternate customer-facing work with accumulated small requests, vary work type for team morale) rather than a single "advance to the next queued item" rule. Fresh customer requests and bugs that surface after shipping don't automatically queue up either: "Let the requests or bugs that just came up compete with everything else at the next betting table to be sure they're strategically important" — they get a deliberate "gentle 'no'" by default, because "the raw ideas that just came in from customer feedback aren't actionable yet. They need to be shaped" (Move On). Sequencing, in other words, is re-decided from a fresh option set every cycle, not read off a queue — "the key to managing capacity is giving ourselves a clean slate with every cycle... only betting one cycle at a time" (The Betting Table).

## Lean Startup (Eric Ries) — pivot-or-persevere and Innovation Accounting

**(a) What ritual/artifact records the gate decision.** Ries's clearest documented instance of the ritual is IMVU's own: "IMVU had a roughly two-month-long development cycle. Each cycle was punctuated by a meeting of our Business Advisory Board (BAB). At this meeting, we would present our goals for the cycle, all the raw results we'd managed to collect, and our conclusions about what was next" (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision). That's a named recurring meeting with three explicit inputs — the pre-committed goal, the raw evidence collected against it, and a stated conclusion — closely mirroring the shape `to-milestone` needs (Evidence Threshold as the pre-committed goal, accumulated evidence, a verdict). More generally, Ries frames the whole practice as an accountability discipline rather than a single meeting format: "To improve entrepreneurial outcomes, and to hold entrepreneurs accountable, we need to focus on the boring stuff: how to measure progress, how to setup milestones, how to prioritize work" (The Lean Startup — Principles). Progress itself is redefined for this purpose — not shipped output, but "validated learning": "Progress in manufacturing is measured by the production of high quality goods. The unit of progress for Lean Startups is validated learning" (The Lean Startup — Principles).

**(b) What the decision options are.** The named decision is explicitly binary at the top level — pivot or persevere — triggered by a comparison against the committed model, not a vague feeling: "When this process of measuring and learning is done correctly, it will be clear that a company is either moving the drivers of the business model or not. If not, it is a sign that it is time to pivot or make a structural course correction to test a new fundamental hypothesis about the product, strategy and engine of growth" (The Lean Startup — Principles). Ries also states an explicit necessity condition narrowing when the *vision itself* — as opposed to a lower-level detail — should be the thing that changes: "If and only if we can't find any market for our current vision is it appropriate to change it" (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision) — i.e., persevere is the default, and only the failure to find *any* market for the current vision authorizes touching the vision layer, a stricter bar than triggering a lower-level pivot. Corroborating context (book-only, not independently fetched as primary web text, per the caveat above): the commonly cited Innovation Accounting sequence names the same two live options once "tuning the engine" stops producing gains — pivot or persevere — as the explicit output of the comparison step.

**(c) How "what's next" gets selected.** Once a pivot is warranted, Ries's own post names three concrete directions rather than leaving "what's next" unstructured: "pivot on customer segment, pivot on customer problem, or pivot on a specific feature" (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision) — each preserves some prior validated learning (the product, the customer, or a proven feature, respectively) while changing the other dimension. This is the clearest primary-source instance found of "what's next after a gate resolves" being a small named menu rather than an open search — structurally similar to Shape Up's small set of rotation heuristics (Place Your Bets) rather than a single default rule. Persevere, by contrast, has no comparable "what's next" menu in the sources checked — the implicit answer is "continue tuning the engine toward the same target," i.e., the same Move continues rather than a new one being selected.

## Amazon Working Backwards (Bryar & Carr) — WBR / MQBR and Correction of Errors

**(a) What ritual/artifact records the gate decision.** Amazon runs this at two nested cadences, both documented on the book's own companion site. The Weekly Business Review (WBR) is the tight-loop version: a report that "starts with the output metrics and then walks through the customer experience journey by department" and can be reviewed "in a one-hour meeting" (Sample WBR Report); its central comparison mechanism plots "the year-over-year (YoY) results" alongside "a goal or plan number" so a metric's trajectory against target is visible at a glance (input metrics & weekly business review). The Monthly/Quarterly Business Review (M/QBR) is the slower, more deliberative cadence: "a focused, detailed, one-hour or more discussion with each significant operating unit and function to ensure they are on track to achieve their goals," with goals sourced from the annual OP1/OP2 planning process and written to SMART criteria ("Specific... Measurable... The criteria for meeting a goal is objective and can be measured using available data") (Quarterly & Monthly Business Reviews). Crucially, Amazon also names a distinct artifact specifically for the "this went wrong" case: significant misses trigger a **Correction of Errors (COE)** review "to determine preventative measures going forward" (Quarterly & Monthly Business Reviews) — a separate, heavier ritual from the routine WBR/M-QBR cadence, reserved for real misses rather than run every cycle regardless of outcome.

**(b) What the decision options are.** Unlike Shape Up's binary ship/no-extension or Lean Startup's pivot/persevere, Amazon's own material frames the review as diagnostic rather than a fixed enumerated menu: the goal is to "understand what is happening with the customer experience and your business" and "gain insights into what kinds of initiatives and events produce both positive and negative results" (input metrics & weekly business review). When a metric misses, the documented response is root-cause narrative before any directional decision: "The Out-Of-Stock (OOS) rate increased 230 bps MoM to 8.2%... The increase was driven by challenges with vendor ABC, whose OOS rate increased from 18.5% in February to 89.2% in March" is the worked example of the expected explanation format (Quarterly & Monthly Business Reviews) — the review's own artifact requires an owner to explain *why*, not just report the number, before the group can act on it.

**(c) How "what's next" gets selected.** Amazon's own material is explicit that the WBR's entire value proposition is that evidence routes directly into next action, not into a report that sits separately from decision-making: reviews should enable "better decision-making at every level of the company" (input metrics & weekly business review), and the WBR itself is built to answer three standing questions every cycle — how the business did, whether it's on track to hit targets, and what customers experienced — which is itself the mechanism for surfacing what needs attention next. For the heavier miss case, the COE is explicitly framed as forward-looking ("preventative measures going forward," Quarterly & Monthly Business Reviews) rather than backward-looking blame — the artifact's job is to determine the next corrective action, not just document the miss.

## OKR check-ins (Google re:Work / John Doerr)

**(a) What ritual/artifact records the gate decision.** Google's own re:Work guide documents an explicit end-of-cycle grading ritual: OKRs are scored on "a 0.0 to 1.0 scale" where "1.0 meaning the objective was fully achieved," with each key result graded and the objective's score computed as "a rough average" of its key results (Set goals with OKRs). Doerr's own companion site names the same ritual with an explicit sequence — grading, then reflecting, then improving — and adds a self-assessment layer on top of the raw number: a PR team's mechanically-computed 0.67 might be revised to 0.9 if the underlying work (e.g., front-page Wall Street Journal coverage) mattered more than the raw count suggests (How to End the OKR Cycle: Grading, Reflecting, and Improving). The grade itself is the recorded artifact — a number plus a red/yellow/green band — rather than free-text narrative, though reflection captures the "why" alongside it.

**(b) What the decision options are.** Both sources use the same three-band structure. Per Google's own guide: "0.7 to 1.0 as green... very close to delivering fully. Or we delivered"; "0.4 to 0.6 are yellow... We made progress but fell short of completion"; "zero and 0.3, that's red. We failed to make significant progress" (Set goals with OKRs, corroborated verbatim on Doerr's own grading page). Doerr's site adds a structural distinction the score alone doesn't carry: **committed** OKRs are binary — "Your Committed OKRs should hit a score of 1.0, or 100 percent. There's no middle ground for these goals; they're either green or red" — while **aspirational** OKRs are graded on a continuum where partial credit is expected and even a middling score can represent real progress (How to End the OKR Cycle). Separately, Doerr's site names the specific, narrow conditions under which the *goal itself* (not just its grade) may be changed mid-cycle — "new information emerges that is significant enough to fundamentally reshape priorities," an external shock ("a global pandemic... outside our control"), or learning "early in the cycle that makes a KR — or even an Objective — irrelevant" — paired with an explicit prohibition: "Never change your OKRs because you're afraid you'll fall short of a goal. That's sandbagging" (When is it Okay to Change an OKR?). A low score, by itself, is not one of the listed legitimate reasons to change the OKR mid-flight.

**(c) How "what's next" gets selected.** Both a hit and a miss feed directly into the next cycle's goal-setting rather than terminating in the score itself. Google's guide states the miss case explicitly: "Low grades should be viewed as data to help refine the next OKRs" (Set goals with OKRs) — reframing a miss as calibration input, not failure requiring escalation. The high-score case has its own corrective logic in the same source: "if someone consistently fully attains their objectives, their OKRs aren't ambitious enough and they need to think bigger" (Set goals with OKRs) — a 1.0 on an aspirational goal is itself a signal to raise the bar next cycle, not simply a win to repeat. Doerr's site frames the mechanism generally as forward-looking rather than a postmortem: reflection asks "How can we get better?" and "collective reflection yields the most value, surfacing tensions that inform the next cycle's goal-setting" (How to End the OKR Cycle) — the grading ritual's stated purpose is explicitly to shape what gets committed to next, not just to record what happened.

## Synthesis

Where the four sources agree: every one of them separates *the trigger for staying the course* from *the trigger for changing course*, and every one of them treats "we're behind" as evidence for the former, not the latter, by default. Shape Up's circuit breaker reads an overrun as a shaping problem to fix in the next pitch, not grounds to keep funding the current one (The Betting Table). Doerr's own site states this as an explicit rule, not just an implication: don't change the goal because it's hard to hit — "that's sandbagging" (whatmatters.com). Ries's vision-pivot condition is the strictest version of the same idea: persevere is the default, and only "if and only if we can't find any market for our current vision" does the top-level target change (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision). All four also agree the review routes directly into the next decision rather than producing a standalone report — Amazon's WBR/COE, Shape Up's betting table, Ries's BAB meeting, and Google's OKR grading are all explicitly described by their own sources as feeding forward, not as terminal audits.

Where they diverge, in ways a `to-milestone` designer would need to pick a side on: **cadence and formality of the artifact** range from Shape Up's undocumented live meeting verdict, through Ries's BAB meeting (goals + raw results + conclusions, but no fixed template found), to Amazon's two explicitly nested cadences (a lightweight weekly number-vs-target check plus a heavier monthly/quarterly narrative-explanation review) and a wholly separate, heavier artifact — the COE — reserved only for significant misses. **Number of live decision options** also varies: Shape Up is strictly binary (ship or don't, no formal "persevere" the way Lean Startup means it — an unfinished bet doesn't get re-funded, it gets reshaped from scratch); Lean Startup is explicitly binary at the top (pivot or persevere) but decomposes "pivot" into a small named menu of directions once triggered; OKRs are the only source with a genuine three-band outcome (green/yellow/red) plus a structural fork between binary "committed" goals and continuum-graded "aspirational" ones. **What licenses touching the threshold itself mid-flight**, as opposed to just grading against it, is explicit only in the OKR and Lean Startup sources — both name a narrow, non-performance-based exception (external shock / significant new information for OKRs; "no market at all" for a Lean Startup vision pivot) and both explicitly forbid revising the target merely because it's proving hard to hit. Shape Up and Amazon's sources don't address this question directly for their own gate mechanism (Shape Up's cool-down decides the *next* bet, not whether to revise the *just-missed* one; Amazon's COE addresses *why* a miss happened, not whether the underlying target was wrong). This gap — under what conditions the Evidence Threshold itself, not just the evidence, may be revisited — is the one place none of the four sources gives a `to-milestone` designer a ready-made, directly-transferable answer.
