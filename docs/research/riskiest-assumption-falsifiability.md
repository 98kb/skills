# How do you tell a genuinely falsifiable "riskiest assumption" from a restated conviction, and what makes its validation plan actually cheap?

Follow-up to `docs/research/vision-roadmap-bridge-artifact.md` Q5, which established that `to-pitch`'s output should carry a mandatory "Riskiest Assumptions & Cheap Validation Plan" field, "in the spirit of Torres's assumption tests," but left open exactly how a grilling-style AI session tests whether a founder's stated assumption is genuinely falsifiable versus just restating their own conviction, and what "cheap" means in practice. This doc does not re-litigate that conclusion — the field stays, in the shape already decided — it closes the open question of how the session should interrogate it. `docs/research/product-planning-pipeline-critique.md` is background only, for the pipeline's constraints (founder-scale team, AI-grilling-session artifacts, no access to real customers during the session, gated revision as the re-entry point for real-world results).

## Q1 — Eric Ries: does he give a concrete test or heuristic for telling a real testable assumption apart from a vague one?

**Verdict:** partially — Ries's own material names the categories (leap-of-faith assumptions, value hypothesis, growth hypothesis) and insists the operational form must be quantitative, but the crispest mechanical heuristic for turning a vague belief into a falsifiable one comes from his own company's published material (Lean Startup Co.), not a single quotable line from Ries about "could this prove me wrong."

- His own site names the target directly: "Beginning with leap-of-faith assumptions that cry out for vigorous testing..." ([theleanstartup.com/book](https://theleanstartup.com/book)) — the framing is explicitly that these assumptions exist to be tested aggressively, not confirmed.
- The two-hypothesis split, in his own company's words: "A value hypothesis tests whether a product delivers value to customers once they're using it, whereas a growth hypothesis tests how new customers discover a product" ([leanstartup.co, 4 Misapplications](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/4-misapplications-of-the-lean-startup-and-how-you-can-avoid-them/)). Splitting "does it work" from "does it spread" is itself a device for narrowing a vague conviction ("this will succeed") into two separately falsifiable claims.
- The clearest heuristic for testability, again from Lean Startup Co.'s own site: "I believe [customers like this] will [behave like this] in [this measurable way]," with the observation that "the most effective [hypotheses] are usually quantitative. That's because they give you a clear way to see whether your assumptions were right" ([leanstartup.co, Lean Startup Method 101](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/lean-startup-method/)). The operationalization of falsifiability here is *quantification*, not an explicit "what would prove this wrong" checklist question.
- The clearest anti-pattern warning in this corpus, also from his own company's site: teams misapply Lean Startup by treating an MVP test as a referendum on whether customers *like* a "mini-version of our ultimate product," when "the goal of the MVP is to begin the process of learning, not end it" — and, tellingly, "successful experiments sometimes show zero customer interest while still validating critical assumptions" ([leanstartup.co, 4 Misapplications](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/4-misapplications-of-the-lean-startup-and-how-you-can-avoid-them/)). A test that only succeeds if customers say they like the thing is testing preference, not the underlying hypothesis — this is the closest primary-source articulation to "restating conviction dressed as a test" found in Ries's own corpus.

## Q2 — Teresa Torres: what technique sharpens a vague assumption into something testable, and what makes a validation test "cheap"?

**Verdict:** Torres's mechanism is twofold and concrete — sharpen via specificity (a story-mapped assumption tied to one step of a customer journey) and de-bias via pre-committed success criteria ("draw a line in the sand") set *before* running the test — and "cheap" is operationalized as a menu of four small activity types plus an explicit time cap, not a vague adjective.

**Sharpening vague into testable:**
- "The more specific your product assumption, the easier it will be to test," and "Be specific. The more specific your assumption is, the smaller and faster the test will be." ([producttalk.org/assumption-testing](https://www.producttalk.org/assumption-testing/))
- The mechanism for getting specific is story mapping: walk through each step of the customer's journey and tie a distinct assumption to each step, rather than stating one global belief about the whole idea. Her own worked example shows the sharpening in action — a vague desirability belief ("our readers want to share this article") sharpens into a specific, measurable viability claim: "Our readers will share articles via SMS with enough non-subscribers (who end up subscribing) to offset the cost of SMS" ([producttalk.org, Five Types of Assumptions](https://www.producttalk.org/2023/10/five-types-of-assumptions/)).
- Her baseline definition keeps the bar low and concrete: "An assumption is a belief that needs to be true in order for your idea to succeed" ([producttalk.org, Assumption glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-assumption/)); an assumption test is "a structured activity designed to evaluate the risk in an underlying assumption that needs to be true for your product idea to succeed" ([producttalk.org, Assumption Test glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-assumption-test/)).
- In the Opportunity Solution Tree itself, assumption tests sit directly under a candidate solution: "Below the solution space are assumption tests. This is how we'll evaluate which solutions will help us best create customer value in a way that drives business value" ([producttalk.org, Opportunity Solution Trees](https://www.producttalk.org/opportunity-solution-trees/)) — the test is scoped to one solution's dependency, not the whole idea, which is itself a sharpening move.

**What makes it "cheap":**
- Four named, low-cost activity types: "prototype tests," "one-question surveys," "data mining," and "research spikes" ([producttalk.org/assumption-testing](https://www.producttalk.org/assumption-testing/)) — a bounded menu, not an open-ended "go run an experiment."
- An explicit ambition on speed: "Do the smallest assumption test that you can complete in the next hour" — cheap means small enough to start immediately, not a multi-week study.

**Differentiating a real test from a leading/confirming one — the key mechanism:**
- "As a team, work to define success upfront, before you run your assumption test. Think through what types of results you would need to see for your solution to succeed and then draw a line in the sand" ([producttalk.org/assumption-testing](https://www.producttalk.org/assumption-testing/)). Pre-committing to a threshold *before* seeing any data is Torres's concrete answer to "how do you know this isn't confirmation-seeking" — if the line in the sand is drawn after the results come in, it isn't a test.
- Her confirmation-bias glossary entry names the failure mode directly: "Confirmation bias is a cognitive bias where your brain is more likely to notice confirming information than disconfirming information—you only see what you want to see," and warns that "even if customers tell you exactly why your idea is flawed, you aren't likely to hear it," and that "the more time and energy you invest in an idea, the more committed you become to it" ([producttalk.org, Confirmation Bias glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-confirmation-bias/)). Her countermeasures: identify likely objections proactively, and actively solicit contradictory perspectives, before running the test — not just while interpreting its results.

## Q3 — Popper-style falsifiability applied practically, by a credible first-party product-practitioner

**Verdict:** found — Ash Maurya (creator of Lean Canvas, author of *Running Lean*) explicitly invokes falsifiability by name on his own company's blog, giving a concrete rewrite template and naming the anti-pattern of an assumption that "always succeeds." He does not cite Popper by name, but the criterion he states is textbook falsifiability, applied practically to startup hypotheses rather than discussed as philosophy.

- The direct instruction: "you need to make them falsifiable or capable of being proven wrong," with a rewrite template for converting a leap of faith into a testable hypothesis: "[Specific testable action] will drive [Expected measurable outcome]." His worked example sharpens a vague claim — "Being considered an expert will drive early adopters to my product" — into a falsifiable one: "Writing a blog post will drive > 100 sign-ups in 2 weeks" ([Ash Maurya, The 7 Habits for Running Highly Effective Lean Startup Experiments](https://medium.com/lean-stack/the-7-habits-for-running-highly-effective-lean-startup-experiments-f1daaa93f85b)).
- Why vagueness defeats the whole exercise: "It is extremely difficult to invalidate a vague theory" — the same point Torres makes about specificity, stated as a falsifiability failure rather than a testing-speed failure.
- The can't-fail anti-pattern, named directly: "If you simply plan on seeing what happens you will always succeed at seeing what happens because something is guaranteed to happen." A test with no pre-declared outcome that would count as failure isn't a test — it's an observation exercise that will always feel like confirmation.
- The timebox-creep anti-pattern: entrepreneurs run an experiment "just a little while longer" hoping for better results, and "those weeks easily turn into months" — cheapness erodes specifically through the absence of a pre-committed stop condition, not just through the initial cost of the test.

## Q4 — Anti-patterns: what goes wrong when a stated "assumption" isn't actually falsifiable

**Verdict:** across all three sources, the failure modes cluster into the same four shapes — restating a preference instead of the underlying hypothesis, a test that structurally cannot fail, no pre-committed threshold or timebox, and confirmation bias in how results get read — and each source names at least one directly, with concrete counter-heuristics.

- **Restates a preference, not the hypothesis.** Ries's own company: an MVP test that only asks whether customers *like* the mini-product is testing preference, not validating "the goal of the MVP is to begin the process of learning, not end it" — proof that this anti-pattern is real: "successful experiments sometimes show zero customer interest while still validating critical assumptions" ([leanstartup.co, 4 Misapplications](https://leanstartup.co/resources/articles/4-misapplications-of-the-lean-startup-and-how-you-can-avoid-them/)).
- **Can't fail by construction.** Maurya, naming it directly: "If you simply plan on seeing what happens you will always succeed at seeing what happens because something is guaranteed to happen" ([Ash Maurya, 7 Habits](https://medium.com/lean-stack/the-7-habits-for-running-highly-effective-lean-startup-experiments-f1daaa93f85b)).
- **No pre-committed threshold or timebox.** Torres's counter-heuristic is to "draw a line in the sand" for success *before* running the test ([producttalk.org/assumption-testing](https://www.producttalk.org/assumption-testing/)); Maurya's corresponding failure mode is timebox creep — "just a little while longer" turning weeks into months ([Ash Maurya, 7 Habits](https://medium.com/lean-stack/the-7-habits-for-running-highly-effective-lean-startup-experiments-f1daaa93f85b)).
- **Cherry-picked / motivated reading of results.** Torres names this as confirmation bias specifically: "you only see what you want to see," compounding as sunk cost grows ("the more time and energy you invest in an idea, the more committed you become to it"), with objections that customers *did* raise going unheard ([producttalk.org, Confirmation Bias glossary](https://www.producttalk.org/glossary-discovery-confirmation-bias/)). Her named countermeasure is to solicit contradictory perspectives and name likely objections proactively, before the test runs — structurally the same fix as pre-committing a threshold: decide what would count as disconfirming *before* you're motivated to explain it away.
- **Too vague to invalidate.** Named independently by both Torres ("the more specific your assumption is, the smaller and faster the test will be") and Maurya ("it is extremely difficult to invalidate a vague theory") — convergent evidence this is the most basic failure mode, prior to and often causing the other three.

## Bottom line

For `to-pitch`'s grilling session, the primary sources converge on a checklist the AI can mechanically apply to each stated "riskiest assumption," without needing access to real customers itself:

1. **Force quantification.** Reject a stated assumption until it fits a form close to Maurya's "[specific testable action] will drive [expected measurable outcome]" or Ries's company's "I believe [X] will [Y] in [measurable way]." If the founder's statement can't be rewritten this way, it's still a conviction, not an assumption — this is the single cheapest falsifiability check available in-session, since it requires no data, only rephrasing.
2. **Ask for the line in the sand, not just the test.** Torres's mechanism is the sharpest tool found for the exact problem this doc was asked to close: before accepting a validation plan, the session should ask "what result, specifically, would prove you wrong?" and pin down that threshold *in the artifact*, before the founder goes and gathers evidence. A validation plan without a pre-committed threshold should be treated as incomplete, not just weak.
3. **Screen for the can't-fail shape.** If the founder's answer to "what would prove you wrong" is something that's guaranteed to happen regardless of outcome (Maurya's diagnostic), or is really a preference question about the artifact itself rather than the underlying value/growth claim (Ries's MVP anti-pattern), the session should push back and ask the founder to restate the assumption one level down — closer to the customer behavior or business outcome it's supposed to stand in for.
4. **Operationalize "cheap" as a menu plus a cap, not a vibe.** Borrow Torres's four test types (prototype test, one-question survey, data mining, research spike) as the menu the session offers the founder to choose from, each implicitly bounded by her "smallest test completable in the next hour" ambition — and pair it with an explicit timebox in the artifact itself, since Maurya's evidence is that the main way cheap tests stop being cheap is time creep after the fact, not an expensive test design up front.
5. **Where this fits the pipeline already decided:** none of this requires the AI to reach a real customer. All four checks are things the session can interrogate purely from how the founder states the assumption and the plan — consistent with `vision-roadmap-bridge-artifact.md`'s finding that validation is a required *field*, populated with a falsifiable claim and a threshold, tested by the human between sessions, with the already-decided gated-revision mechanism as the point where the actual result (proved / disproved against the pre-committed line in the sand) comes back upstream.
