# What do "wedge," "strategic choices," "sequencing," and "evidence-per-bet" actually mean in the primary sources `to-roadmap` borrows them from?

Research for `to-roadmap`, the third skill in the `to-vision → to-pitch → to-roadmap → to-milestone` pipeline (see `docs/research/product-planning-pipeline-critique.md` and `docs/research/vision-roadmap-bridge-artifact.md` for the pipeline's shape and this doc's citation precedent). `to-roadmap` consumes exactly one approved pitch artifact (Problem, Appetite, Solution sketch, Riskiest Assumptions & Cheap Validation Plan, Rabbit Holes, No-gos, Open Questions — per the `to-pitch` reshape the prior doc argued for) and is where the milestone map's design Notes place the strategy layer: wedge, strategic choices, constraints, differentiation, ordering logic, evidence-per-bet. There is deliberately no separate `to-strategy` skill. Already decided and not re-litigated here: `to-roadmap` reads only the pitch, never re-fetches the vision; its output is a living, ordered sequence of Shape-Up-scale "Moves" headed by an explicit "Wedge" statement; each Move carries a lightweight "Evidence Threshold" that `to-roadmap` states and `to-milestone` later judges; the roadmap revises in place as evidence returns; cross-roadmap portfolio/betting-table sequencing is explicitly out of scope for this skill.

This doc investigates five questions about the vocabulary that design intends to use, against first-party sources only (the practitioner's own book companion site, own company site, or own blog — never a secondary summary). Several searches for a single, clean primary-source "coinage" of "wedge" turned up multiple independent, sometimes conflicting usages rather than one authoritative origin — that plurality is itself a finding, reported in Q1 rather than resolved into one preferred definition.

## Q1 — What does "wedge" mean and where does it actually come from?

**Verdict: no single practitioner coined "wedge" as a term of art — it names at least two materially different strategies depending on which primary source you read, neither of which is where the word itself first appears; of the three named candidates, Play Bigger provably does not use the word, Thiel's own material describes the mechanic without ever using the word, and only Dunford uses "wedge" in her own primary material, but in a narrower sense than the market-expansion story the pipeline's chartering implies.**

**Thiel/Zero to One describes the mechanic, never the word.** Blake Masters' Class 4 notes — Thiel's own 2012 Stanford lecture, later condensed into *Zero to One* — state the core claim directly: "Start with a really small market and take over the whole market, then find ways to expand the market," illustrated with Amazon (books → broad e-commerce), eBay (Pez dispensers and Beanie Babies → general auctions), and PayPal (roughly 20,000 eBay power sellers, 25–30% penetration within months → built outward from that foothold). The word "wedge" does not appear anywhere in this primary text.

**Chris Dixon's own blog is the closest thing to an actual primary-source coinage found for the specific word, and even he hedges it as pre-existing jargon.** His December 2010 post opens: "One common strategy for establishing this initial relationship is what is sometimes known as the 'thin edge of the wedge' strategy (aka the 'tip of the spear' strategy)" — explicitly signaling he's naming, not originating, a term already circulating. His own framing: "Good startups... think about the whole wedge from the start. They build an initial user base with simple features and then quickly iterate," illustrated with Instagram/Picplz (photo sharing filled a gap other apps ignored), Delicious (cloud bookmarking before social features), and OpenTable (restaurant CRM terminals first, consumer product second, once inventory existed). Dixon treats this as a *product-scoping* tactic and explicitly separates it from a companion post — see below — on *market-sequencing*.

**That companion post is where Dixon explicitly credits Geoffrey Moore**, not Thiel: "what Geoffrey Moore calls the bowling pin strategy" (Dixon's own words, in his August 2010 post) — find a niche where "the chicken-and-egg problem is more easily overcome," then "hop from that niche to other niches and eventually to the broader market," choosing niches for "spillover potential into other niches" (Facebook: Harvard → other colleges via existing friend connections → the general public). This is the beachhead/bowling-pin lineage from *Crossing the Chasm* — a different primary lineage than Thiel's, arrived at independently and only bridged by Dixon's own writing, not by either Thiel or Moore citing each other. (Moore's own companion site could not be located as a first-party source at the sourcing bar this doc uses — geoffreymoore.com resolves to an unrelated real-estate agent's site — so Moore's own account is represented here only through Dixon's own citation of it, not verified directly against Moore's own words.)

**April Dunford does use "wedge" in her own primary material — but she means something narrower.** Her own podcast, *Positioning with April Dunford*, frames the choice as one of three positioning options for a company's own existing product line: lead with "a wedge product, a platform, or a suite of products." This is a single-company, multiple-products-already-built question ("which of our own products do we sell first, before cross-selling the rest") — not the Thiel/Dixon/Moore story of entering a genuinely new market and later expanding into a different, larger one. Her own blog archive (20 posts reviewed directly, including "Positioning vs Strategy vs Vision" and "How To Select A Target Market") does not otherwise use "wedge" as a defined term. **Two different concepts share one word across these sources — that ambiguity, not just the sourcing question, is itself worth flagging for whatever `to-roadmap` writes into its Wedge statement.**

**Play Bigger does not use "wedge" anywhere in its own material.** Their own site (playbigger.com/categorydesign) lays out a complete, distinct vocabulary — Category King, Lightning Strike, Point of View, the Define/Develop/Dominate Category Lifecycle, Strike Ops Flywheel, Category Blueprint — with no overlap with "wedge." Ruled out as a source for the term.

**Marty Cagan/SVPG does not use "wedge" anywhere in the material searched either** — a direct site-scoped search returned no hits. This matters because SVPG is the pipeline's primary strategy-language source elsewhere (Q2 below): "wedge" is being imported from an entirely separate practitioner lineage (VC/GTM blogs) than the one grounding the rest of `to-roadmap`'s vocabulary.

**Lenny Rachitsky's own newsletter (his own analysis, confirmed not an interview transcript) gives the cleanest single synthesis found**, matching the Thiel/Dixon/Moore lineage rather than Dunford's: "a strategy to win a large market by initially capturing (1) a tiny part of a larger market or (2) a large part of a small adjacent market," composed of "the right initial product and the right initial market" chosen together, not sequentially. He cites Sarah Tavel (Benchmark) on the need for "extraordinary focus on a specific persona/use case to start," and Olivier Pomel (Datadog's CEO) on cases where a wedge isn't necessary at all — infrastructure software, in Pomel's account — a caveat directly relevant to Q5.

## Q2 — What do practitioners mean by "strategic choices," as distinct from feature prioritization?

**Verdict: Cagan draws this line explicitly and repeatedly across the SVPG Product Strategy series — strategy is a small number of hard, insight-driven choices about which problems a team is given, prior to and structurally different from ranking a backlog of already-decided features or requests.**

- **Strategy precedes and is categorically different from delivery work.** "product strategy helps us decide what problems to solve; product discovery helps us figure out the tactics that can actually solve the problems; and product delivery builds that solution" (Product Strategy – Overview). Prioritization, by contrast, typically operates only on the third of these — a list of things already decided to be worth doing.
- **Choice means exclusion, not ranking.** "Choices means focus. Deciding what few things you really need to do, and therefore all the things you won't do" (Product Strategy – Overview), reinforced by Rumelt (quoted by Cagan, Product Strategy – Focus): "Good strategy works by focusing energy and resources on one, or a very few, pivotal objectives whose accomplishment will lead to a cascade of favorable outcomes." Cagan's own diagnosis of the failure mode: organizations claim to prioritize but actually run 20–30 simultaneous initiatives instead of 2–3.
- **The Pandora case is Cagan's own direct evidence that shipping-and-prioritizing is not the same as having a strategy.** He cites it as an organization that shipped many prioritized features yet exhibited "a complete lack of product strategy" (Product Strategy – Insights) — prioritization was present, strategy was not.
- **Strategic choices are insight-sourced, not request-sourced.** Cagan names four concrete inputs: quantitative data and live-data testing, qualitative customer research, enabling-technology possibilities, and industry/competitive trends (Product Strategy – Insights) — a different input pipeline than a stakeholder feature-request queue.
- **Strategy converts into "problems," not features, handed to teams.** "whether you give your product teams features to build, or problems to solve" is, per Cagan, the actual fork in the road (Product Strategy – Actions); the empowered-team model gives problems (Objectives) plus a way to measure solving them (Key Results), not a ranked feature list.
- **This is consistent with the pipeline's existing citation of "The Alternative to Roadmaps"** (already cited in `product-planning-pipeline-critique.md`): business objectives are prioritized at the product-organization level, OKR-style, specifically so competing teams' *outcomes* — not their *feature backlogs* — get stack-ranked against one another.

## Q3 — What primary sources describe the order bets get placed in over time?

**Verdict: the clearest sequencing logic comes from the Moore/Dixon bowling-pin lineage and from Shape Up's own cycle-by-cycle betting mechanism — both describe sequencing as emergent and evidence-conditioned rather than planned upfront across multiple objectives at once; Cagan's own most roadmap-adjacent piece, checked directly, does not actually supply this — a real gap in that lineage relative to the other two.**

- **Bowling-pin/beachhead sequencing (via Dixon's own account of Moore's model):** the first niche is chosen not just for its own merit but for "spillover potential into other niches" — the ordering logic is baked into the *first* choice, not decided independently at each step. Subsequent niches are reached by following existing bridges (Facebook: Harvard → colleges with actual friend-connections to Harvard → general public), and you "hop... only after dropping that first pin" — sequential, not parallel, expansion.
- **Shape Up sequences one bet at a time, decided fresh each cycle, not planned in advance.** "Every bet on HEY was placed one at a time" (Place Your Bets). The betting table looks only at "pitches from the last six weeks — or any pitches that somebody purposefully revived and lobbied for again" (Place Your Bets) — there is no standing backlog being worked down in order.
- **What comes next is explicitly conditioned on what came before, as a distinct sequencing rule of its own:** "The kind of project we want to do next can depend on which projects we've done recently" (Place Your Bets) — Shape Up names concrete rotation logic: alternate visible customer-facing work with accumulated smaller requests, and rotate work types to manage team morale, rather than a fixed priority order.
- **The circuit breaker feeds back into sequencing, not just scope:** "if a project doesn't finish in the six weeks, it means we did something wrong in the shaping" (The Betting Table) — an overrun is read as a signal about the *next* shaping/betting decision, not merely a scheduling failure to patch by extending the current one.
- **Cagan's own "Alternative to Roadmaps" does not, on direct re-checking, supply comparable temporal-sequencing guidance.** It states objectives should be prioritized and worked "highest business value items first," but contains no explicit mechanism for *when* or in what order multiple objectives get tackled relative to each other across quarters — it answers *what* to prioritize (outcomes over features) without answering *in what order over time*, which is a real, checkable gap in that source relative to the bowling-pin and Shape Up material above.

## Q4 — What does "evidence-per-bet" look like in practice?

**Verdict: Cagan's dual-track discovery/delivery model and Shape Up's cool-down reflection are the two clearest primary-source mechanisms, and both route evidence directly into the next decision rather than treating it as a separate reporting exercise — but Cagan's own most relevant piece on measurement, read directly, stops short of describing the resequencing loop explicitly.**

- **Evidence gates the delivery commitment, not just the post-launch verdict.** Cagan: "the purpose of product discovery is to make sure we have some evidence that when we ask the engineers to build production-quality software, it won't be a wasted effort" (Discovery vs. Delivery) — evidence (customer interviews, prototypes, live-data testing with volunteer customers) is gathered *before* the bet is fully funded with delivery resources, not only measured after.
- **Objectives/Key Results are built to make in-flight adjustment possible, not just end-of-quarter grading.** "The Objectives are the customer or business problem we need solved, and the key results are how we measure the progress" (Team Objectives – Empowerment); teams should "immediately understand if they are solving the problem, and make adjustments based on the data" (Product Strategy – Actions).
- **Gap in Cagan's own material, found by direct re-check:** "Outcomes Are Hard" establishes how to define and measure an outcome but, read directly, "doesn't explicitly detail how measurement data informs strategic recalibration or prioritization of future problems" — it's the weakest of the SVPG sources on the actual evidence-to-resequencing mechanic, despite being the piece most directly on point by title.
- **Shape Up's cool-down is where evidence explicitly becomes the next decision, at the tightest cadence of any source here (six weeks):** "Each six weeks we learn what's working and what isn't, what's important and what's not" (Bets, Not Backlogs); the two-week unscheduled cool-down exists specifically to "consider what to do next," and if a cycle's results disappoint, "we could define a very different project" for the next cycle rather than continuing the same trajectory (The Betting Table).
- **For continuity with the prior doc's Q3 finding:** Torres's opportunity solution tree is revisited "every three to four customer interviews" — the same evidence-into-resequencing pattern as Cagan and Shape Up, but at a faster, interview-driven cadence rather than a fixed cycle length. All three sources converge on the same shape (evidence closes a loop back into the *next* sequencing decision, not a terminal report), differing only in cadence and formality.

## Q5 — Does wedge/beachhead thinking fit downstream of a single Shape-Up-style pitch, with no cross-roadmap portfolio view?

**Verdict: there is a real, unresolved tension, and it should be flagged rather than papered over — every primary source's actual worked examples of "wedge"/beachhead expansion describe a trajectory across what this pipeline would treat as separate, currently-unconnected pitches, which is exactly the cross-roadmap view `to-roadmap` has already been scoped to not have.**

Every source examined for Q1 that tells a wedge/beachhead story pairs the *narrow starting point* with an *implied or explicit adjacent target*, and treats picking both together as the actual strategic act:

- Dixon's own framing is explicit that the choice isn't sequential-and-separate: "think about the whole wedge from the start" (thin-edge-of-the-wedge post).
- The bowling-pin niche is chosen partly *for* its "spillover potential into other niches" (bowling-pin post) — the first pin's value is partly defined by what it unlocks next.
- Rachitsky's own definition names both halves as one unit: "the right initial product and the right initial market."
- The worked examples across every source cluster on the same shape — PayPal's eBay-power-seller foothold expanding into a general payments platform (Thiel/Masters), Amazon's books expanding into general e-commerce (Thiel/Masters), Facebook's Harvard beachhead expanding college-by-college into the general public (Dixon/Moore), Salesforce's Sales Cloud wedge expanding into Marketing Cloud and Service Cloud as separate products (the Dunford-adjacent usage found during search), Twilio's SMS API expanding into a full communications platform (Rachitsky). In every one of these, the "expand to" half is a *different product or market* than the "start with" half — not a later, still-in-bounds slice of the same originally-approved problem statement.
- Play Bigger's Category Design vocabulary reinforces this at the extreme end: its own material talks about "mobilizing the entire company" and a "Category Blueprint... mapping people, processes, and products" — an explicitly whole-company, multi-product view, not a single-bet view.

**This is where the tension with the pipeline's own already-decided constraints sits.** `to-roadmap` reads exactly one approved pitch, never re-fetches the vision, and cross-roadmap/portfolio sequencing is explicitly out of scope for this skill. But the vision is precisely the pipeline artifact that — per `product-planning-pipeline-critique.md` — can fan out into many independent pitches/roadmaps, never merged. A Wedge statement written inside a single `to-roadmap` run therefore has no access to: (a) sibling pitches spawned from the same vision that might be the actual "adjacent market" a wedge trajectory is aiming at, or (b) any vision-level view of what "adjacent" would even mean beyond this one pitch's own bounded Problem/Solution sketch. This is the same structural gap `product-planning-pipeline-critique.md` already flagged under its Q4 ("one PRD → exactly one roadmap, never merged" breaking under cross-PRD resource contention) — it resurfaces here specifically because "wedge," in every primary source's own worked example, is a cross-artifact claim by construction, not a within-one-bet claim.

Two structurally different ways the sources leave this — surfaced, not resolved, per this doc's brief:

1. **A narrower reading stays inside one pitch's own bounds:** "expand" could mean later Moves within the *same* roadmap, still inside the one approved pitch's own Problem/Solution scope — i.e., the wedge is the first Move, and the "expand" is Move 2, 3, 4 addressing adjacent parts of the *same already-approved* problem space. Nothing in the sources rules this out; it just isn't the reading any of the worked examples actually depict.
2. **The literal reading every source's own examples use is cross-product/cross-market**, which a single-pitch-scoped `to-roadmap` run cannot substantiate — it could assert the "start narrow" half of a Wedge statement with full support from its one pitch, but the "expand to X" half would be speculation about artifacts (sibling pitches, the vision) it is not designed to see.

Notably, Cagan/SVPG — the source grounding the rest of `to-roadmap`'s vocabulary per Q2 — never uses "wedge" and keeps its own strategy language scoped to "the team['s]" problem rather than a multi-market expansion narrative, which is arguably the one lineage here that *doesn't* structurally assume portfolio visibility. That SVPG silence on "wedge" specifically, combined with every source that *does* use the term assuming a multi-artifact view, is the clearest evidence that "wedge" and "strategic choices" are being drawn from two lineages with different scope assumptions, not one coherent one.
