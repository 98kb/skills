# What is a "vision" artifact, and where does it end and pitch/strategy begin?

Follow-up to `docs/research/product-planning-pipeline-critique.md` and `docs/research/vision-roadmap-bridge-artifact.md`, which researched the pitch/roadmap/milestone stages and deliberately left the upstream `to-vision` stage unresearched. This doc fills that gap: `to-vision` is an interactive grilling session that turns a founder's raw idea into a vision document, which then fans out into one or more `to-pitch` pitches (Problem, Appetite, Solution sketch, Riskiest Assumptions & Cheap Validation Plan, Rabbit Holes, No-gos, Open Questions — shape already decided by the prior doc). Vision is the first stage; nothing is upstream of it. All sources below are first-party (the practitioner's own site/book/blog), per the same sourcing bar as the prior two docs — secondary summaries were used only to locate primary URLs, never cited as evidence.

## Q1 — What is the actual structure/content of a "vision" artifact in each source?

**Verdict: Cagan is the only source with an explicit field-level breakdown; Amazon and Ries treat vision as a role/property (stability, customer-back framing) rather than a document with sections; Amplitude's North Star is the closest thing to a measurable-target companion piece, not a vision document itself.**

- **Cagan (SVPG) — the only itemized structure found.** A strong vision must address nine essential factors: "Business Objectives and Constraints, Customer Problems, Critical Insights, Enabling Technologies, Industry Trends, Competitive Landscape, Go To Market Considerations, Our Own Capabilities, Organizational Impacts" (Product Vision FAQ). Time horizon: "typically somewhere between 2 and 5 years out" for software, "5-10 years out" for hardware/device companies (Vision vs. Strategy; Product Vision FAQ). Form is explicitly not a spec: "a persuasive piece," often built as a "visiontype" — a storyboard, narrative, or prototype rather than prose bullets (Vision vs. Strategy; The Power of Visiontypes). Authorship is singular and explicit: "the product vision is the explicit responsibility of the head of product" (Product Vision FAQ).
- **Amazon PR/FAQ — vision has no distinct section or artifact; it is diffused into the press release's customer-back framing.** Direct check of the official template confirms this: fetching workingbackwards.com's PR/FAQ instructions page found "vision is not mentioned as a formal component" — the listed sections are Heading, Subheading, Summary Paragraph, Problem Paragraph, Solution Paragraph(s), Quotes & Getting Started, then External/Internal FAQ. "Vision" appears only as descriptive language *about* the press release's effect ("your customer-centric vision"), not as a labeled field or a separate upstream document (Working Backwards PR/FAQ Process; PR/FAQ Instructions & Template).
- **Ries — vision is a property (stability), not a document with fields.** His own pre-book blog post is explicit that surviving pivots, not section headings, is what defines vision: successful startups "change directions but stay grounded in what they've learned. They keep one foot in the past and place one foot in a new possible future," whereas failing startups "simply jump outright from one vision to something completely different" (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision). His own site's Principles page treats vision as something to be "test[ed]... continuously," not authored once as a static document (The Lean Startup — Principles). Note: the commonly cited "Vision-Strategy-Product pyramid" is from the book itself, which was not independently fetchable as primary web text here — treated as corroborating context per the same caveat the first research doc applied to *INSPIRED*, not cited as a standalone primary quote.
- **Amplitude's North Star — a measurable companion artifact, not a vision document itself, but structurally the sharpest of the sources found.** A North Star Metric should be something "someone... can read... and understand your company's product strategy and vision — at least at a high level" (Good vs. Bad North Star Metric) — i.e., Amplitude explicitly positions the metric as a *derivative* of vision, not the vision itself.
- **Paul Graham — no structured vision artifact at all.** "Before the Startup" argues against front-loading grand vision work pre-launch: founders should build domain expertise and "make something people want," with the essay explicitly steering away from long-range strategizing before starting (Before the Startup). No YC primary source found proposes a vision document format; this appears to be a genuine gap in the YC corpus relative to Cagan/Amazon/Ries.

## Q2 — Where does each source draw the boundary between vision and strategy/roadmap/pitch?

**Verdict: every source that draws this boundary at all puts it in the same place — vision names the destination and the customer/market thesis, strategy and roadmap are the sequence of bets to get there — and none of them let vision touch scope, time-boxing, or solution detail, which maps cleanly onto keeping `to-vision` out of `to-pitch`'s Appetite/Solution-sketch/Rabbit-Holes/No-gos territory.**

- Cagan states the boundary directly, twice over: "the product vision doesn't usually change much at all," while "the product strategy changes frequently (at least yearly if not quarterly, based on new data and insights)" (Product Vision FAQ). Structurally: "vision establishes *where* to go; strategy determines *what problems to solve* along the path" (Vision vs. Strategy). He separately distinguishes vision from company mission — mission is "a pithy slogan" answering *why the org exists* company-wide, vision is *product*-specific and forward-looking, serving as "a North Star for the product organization" (Product Vision vs. Mission) — relevant because `to-vision`'s output should not collapse into a mission-statement genericism either.
- Cagan also anchors vision *before* product-market fit, not overlapping it: "while the Product Vision inspires Product Market Fit, the Product Market Fit precedes the Product Vision and is usually the first real test of the Product Vision" (Product Market Fit vs. Product Vision). This is a clean upstream/downstream ordering: vision → (search for) product-market fit → strategy/roadmap execution toward the vision.
- Cagan's vision-pivot vs. discovery-pivot split further sharpens the boundary: "A vision pivot puts you on a path to a different business with a different vision," while "a discovery pivot moves you closer to realizing your product vision" — monetization models, growth channels, and even features are explicitly discovery/strategy-level and can flex "while staying committed to the underlying vision" (Vision Pivots vs. Discovery Pivots). This is the clearest evidence that solution-level detail (which `to-pitch` already owns via Solution sketch/Rabbit Holes/No-gos) is *not* vision territory even in Cagan's own framing.
- Amazon draws the boundary implicitly rather than explicitly, but consistently: the press release *is* the customer-facing vision statement, and the FAQ (external + internal) is where problem-specific, solution-specific, and objection-specific detail lives (PR/FAQ Instructions & Template). Since this pipeline already assigns problem/solution/riskiest-assumptions to `to-pitch`, Amazon's own artifact split suggests vision-as-input should stay at the press-release altitude (who, what future, why it matters) and hand off FAQ-level specificity entirely to `to-pitch`.
- Ries draws the same boundary via what survives a pivot: vision survives, strategy (business model, roadmap, "point of view about partners and competitors," beliefs about who the customer is) is what changes (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision; corroborated by the VSP framing in the book). Solution/appetite/rabbit-holes are unambiguously strategy/tactics-layer in this framing, not vision-layer.
- Amplitude's framework explicitly treats vision as upstream of and distinct from the metric/strategy layer it's selling: the North Star Metric is a "leading indicator" that should be readable as a proxy for vision, not a restatement of it (Good vs. Bad North Star Metric) — again vision sits one level above any measurable, scoped artifact.

**Net for `to-vision`'s scope:** every source agrees vision = the future being pursued + who it's for + why it matters, stated at a durable altitude. None of them let vision include a solution shape, a time-budget, or an assumptions-to-test list — those are unanimously one layer down, which is exactly the layer this pipeline has already assigned to `to-pitch`. This is strong independent confirmation that keeping `to-vision`'s output free of Problem/Appetite/Solution/Riskiest-Assumptions content is correct, not just non-duplicative by fiat.

## Q3 — What makes a vision "sharp" (falsifiable/testable/specific) vs. vague and inspirational?

**Verdict: Cagan gives the clearest anti-pattern with a real example; Amplitude gives the clearest positive criteria for measurability; the common failure mode named across sources is the same — a vision that could describe any company.**

- Cagan's own example of a bad vision, verbatim: "We will make customers' lives better through automation" — his verdict: "nice but not helpful," because it doesn't "give the audience a real sense of *how* you will make their lives better" (Product Vision FAQ). The nine-factor checklist (business objectives, customer problems, critical insights, enabling technologies, industry trends, competitive landscape, go-to-market, capabilities, org impact) functions as his own sharpness test — a vision that doesn't ground itself in specific insights, trends, or technologies is exactly the kind of interchangeable-slogan vision he's warning against.
- Amplitude's criteria, stated as explicit pass/fail conditions rather than examples, translate directly into a falsifiability test: a good North Star must be something "you can influence or change" (actionable) and something for which "you need to be able to collect the appropriate data to track and communicate" (measurable) — and it must be a *leading* indicator, not a lagging one, with the explicit warning: "if your North Star isn't directionally indicating where your revenue is going, then it's time to revise it" (Good vs. Bad North Star Metric). Vanity metrics are named as the specific failure mode to avoid. While this is framed as metric selection rather than vision-writing, it is the sharpest "how do you know if this is testable" language found in any source here, and maps well onto a grilling-question test: *can this vision claim be wrong, and would you know if it were?*
- Cagan's mission-vs-vision distinction doubles as a sharpness test: a mission statement ("organize the world's information") is deliberately generic and durable across any strategy; a vision fails if it reads the same way — generic enough to survive contact with any market (Product Vision vs. Mission). If a vision could be mistaken for a mission statement, it's too vague.
- Ries's implicit sharpness test is falsifiability-through-experimentation rather than a wording rule: a vision is being tested continuously via build-measure-learn ("providing tools to test a vision continuously" — The Lean Startup, Principles), meaning a vision that can't be operationalized into experiments isn't sharp enough to act on. This is consistent with Cagan's factor-based test (specific insights/trends/tech are exactly what make a vision testable) and Amplitude's measurability test.

**Composite sharpness test for a future `to-vision` grilling tree:** (1) does it name a specific customer and a specific future state, not a platitude interchangeable with any competitor's vision (Cagan's bad-example test); (2) does it commit to at least one of the nine grounding factors — an insight, a trend, a technology bet — rather than staying at the "make lives better" altitude (Cagan's checklist); (3) is there something a reader could point to and say "this didn't happen" (Amplitude's leading-indicator/actionable/measurable test, Ries's continuous-testing framing).

## Q4 — Is vision a single fixed document, or does it evolve/get revisited over time?

**Verdict: durable-but-not-frozen, and every source that addresses this converges on the same shape — vision changes rarely and only under specific triggering conditions, unlike strategy/roadmap which are expected to change routinely.**

- Cagan is explicit about relative cadence: "the product strategy changes frequently (at least yearly if not quarterly...), the product vision doesn't usually change much at all" (Product Vision FAQ) — not "never," but a different order of magnitude of stability than strategy or roadmap.
- Cagan's vision-pivot/discovery-pivot split names the actual trigger condition for revising vision: only when the underlying market thesis is falsified — customers "just don't care enough" — should the vision itself change; day-to-day learning should produce discovery pivots that leave the vision intact (Vision Pivots vs. Discovery Pivots). He also warns explicitly against reacting to vision on too short a timescale: don't abandon a vision after "a few weeks or even a few months" — validation takes "many months" (same source). His guiding maxim, quoting Bezos: "Stubborn on the vision, and flexible on the details" (Vision vs. Strategy).
- Ries's own framing treats a vision as something that gets *tested*, not rewritten, by default: "companies can create order not chaos by providing tools to test a vision continuously" (Principles) — implying persistence through iteration is normal, and only a deliberate "vision pivot" (not incremental learning) changes it, consistent with his blog's warning against startups that "jump outright from one vision to something completely different" (Pivot, don't jump to a new vision).
- Amazon's PR/FAQ doesn't describe routine vision revision at all (there is no separate vision artifact to revise — see Q1) — the only revisitation behavior documented in the primary source is for shelved/rejected *ideas* being "revisited in future quarters or years" (per the prior research doc's citation of the same source), which is a different mechanism (re-litigating a rejected idea) than living-document maintenance of an approved vision.

**For this pipeline's revision-propagation mechanism:** vision should sit at the *most* conservative end of that mechanism — it should be the artifact least frequently touched by upstream revision-propagation, and should only be reopened when a pitch/roadmap/milestone surfaces evidence that falsifies the vision's core market thesis (a genuine "vision pivot" per Cagan), not on every downstream learning. Treating vision with the same revision cadence as pitch or roadmap would contradict every source that addresses cadence at all.

## Bottom line

Strongest recommendation: **`to-vision`'s grilling session should force the founder to commit to Cagan's specific, falsifiable grounding (a named customer, a named future state, and at least one concrete insight/trend/technology bet the founder is willing to be wrong about) while explicitly refusing any Appetite-, Solution-, or Assumption-level detail — and the output should carry a stated revision trigger (what would constitute a "vision pivot," per Cagan) rather than an implicit assumption that it's rewritten whenever a pitch changes.** This is the one design choice every source converges on: vision is deliberately shallow on solution/scope (unanimous across Cagan, Amazon, Ries) but deliberately deep on specificity of customer/insight/future-state (Cagan's factor list, Amplitude's measurability test) — and it changes far less often, and only for a named reason, than anything downstream of it (Cagan's pivot taxonomy, Ries's "test continuously" framing). Concretely: `to-vision`'s output should be short — closer to Amazon's one-page press release altitude than to a PR/FAQ's six pages — and every future `to-pitch` run should be checked against it for consistency, not the reverse.
