# Vision

## Vision Statement

Every used lab instrument carries a verifiable service history, so PIs buy refurbished by default.

## Customer & Problem

The customer is the principal investigator — someone like Dr. Hana Lindqvist, who runs a six-person academic lab on a four-year grant and needs a benchtop centrifuge this quarter. New costs eleven thousand dollars and consumes her entire equipment line. When she looks used, she finds eBay listings with no service history, a regional dealer who won't quote without a phone call, and a departmental mailing list.

The breakage isn't price discovery — it's that she has no way to tell whether a used unit was actually maintained. So she gives up, buys new, and cuts something else to pay for it.

## Future State

Hana checks the resale market first, the way you'd check a used car listing with a full service record. The unit's actual maintenance history is attached to its serial number and visible before she commits. She buys it in an afternoon at a third of the price of new and puts the difference into people instead of hardware. Equipment stops being scrapped at the end of every grant cycle.

## Grounding Insight

The reason used lab equipment doesn't sell is provenance, not price. Nobody can prove a unit was serviced, so buyers assume the worst and pay for new.

What has changed is that service contracts on this class of instrument have gone almost entirely digital and manufacturer-held in the last few years. For the first time, a maintenance history can be attached to a specific serial number and travel with the machine when it's sold. The bet is that verifiable service history — not a lower price — is what unlocks this market.

This is wrong if labs turn out to buy used at scale without caring about provenance, or if manufacturers lock that history down and refuse to let it move with the unit.

## Why Us / Why Now

Five years as a field service engineer for a major instrument manufacturer, having personally opened several thousand of these machines. That means knowing what a service record has to contain before a buyer will believe it — which fields matter, which are theatre, and where records lie. A generalist marketplace doesn't know what to ask for.

Manufacturers hold the data but have no incentive to help a machine get resold instead of replaced. The digital-service-record shift is recent enough that nobody has built on top of it yet, and that window is the "why now."

## Additional Grounding

Many universities now carry procurement targets that reward buying refurbished, and none of them have a supplier who can actually meet them. There is a buyer with a mandate and no way to satisfy it. Procurement is a tailwind that makes the PI's decision easier to get signed off — not the buyer being won directly.

## Vision Pivot Trigger

If a few hundred provenance-verified units are listed and they don't sell measurably faster, or for measurably more, than the same models with no service history, the premise is wrong. It's a clean comparison, runnable within a year.
